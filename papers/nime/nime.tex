\begin{abstract}
REMORA is a martial-arts staff (b\^o) played as a musical instrument. An inertial sensor strapped
near one end streams over Wi-Fi to an audio plugin, which turns spins, turns of the body, and
thrusts into sound. We wanted features that belong to the staff rather than to IMUs in general, so
the plugin tracks the plane and direction of a spin, which of two floor lines the staff is working
along, how many laps a spin has lasted, and, experimentally, thrusts along the staff. A three-pose
calibration makes all of this independent of how the enclosure is rotated on the staff. The mapping
from features to sound is a small dataflow patch that can be edited inside the plugin while audio
runs. We use it as an authoring tool in rehearsal, not as something the performer rewires mid-form.
We also report what went wrong. A pilot with ten mostly novice players liked the instrument but
found thrusts poorly detected. Tracing that led us to three defects: the analyzer ran per audio
block, gravity was subtracted in the wrong frame, and the orientation filter lost tilt during fast
spins. We describe the fixes and check them in simulation. Measurements on the real staff and
sessions with trained players are still to come.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
The first author trains with the b\^o and also makes music, and REMORA started as an attempt to
make one practice audible through the other. The b\^o is a staff of about 1.5\,m. It is held in two
hands and swung, spun, and thrust through techniques that are taught as forms (\emph{kata}). Most
inertial-sensor Digital Musical Instruments (DMIs) shown at New Interfaces for Musical Expression
(NIME) are small: something held in one hand or worn on the body. A staff is different. It is long,
it has real rotational inertia, it reaches far from the body, and a trained player already controls
a large, codified set of movements with it.

We did not know at the start which of those movements should make which sounds, and we still do
not fully know. Our first answer was to write each gesture-to-sound mapping as its own C++ class.
That made every experiment a rebuild. After eleven classes we noticed that they kept reusing the
same arithmetic, the same smoothing, and the same few stateful tricks, so we moved the mapping into
a dataflow patch that is edited inside the audio plugin itself. Visual patching is not new
(\S\ref{sec:related}). For us it is the tool we use to try out and revise the staff's vocabulary.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{claim:features} Motion features designed for a long, swung object: spin plane and
direction, a two-state facing that needs no magnetometer, and a continuous lap count, all computed
once per sensor packet from the gyroscope in a calibrated world frame (\S\ref{sec:features}). An
axial thrust detector is included but has not yet been validated on the real staff.
\item \label{claim:calibration} A three-pose calibration that removes the effect of how the
enclosure is rotated on the staff, including the zero of roll (\S\ref{sec:calibration}).
\item \label{claim:dag} A mapping layer inside the plugin, built as a typed directed acyclic graph
(DAG) over a catalog of 69 node types, in which all eleven earlier hand-coded mappings and four new
ones are expressed as presets (\S\ref{sec:mapping-arch}, \S\ref{sec:named-mappings}).
\end{enumerate}
We also describe three defects that the pilot study exposed and how we fixed them
(\S\ref{sec:fixes}), because two of them would affect any staff- or club-like instrument that
reuses a stock orientation filter. Table~\ref{tab:claims-evidence} lists the evidence for each
contribution and what is still untested.

\subsection{Scope}
We designed REMORA for a soloist with some staff or martial-arts background who plays a set built
from presets. The presets are graded. \preset{Simple} needs only tilt and roll. The facing- and
spin-driven \preset{Martial Momentum} and \preset{Azimut Kinetic} only make sense if the player can
already spin and turn with a staff. The patch editor is for whoever prepares the set: the performer
in rehearsal, or a second person at the computer during a performance. Ensemble use is out of scope.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Rod, Baton, and Swung Controllers}
The \textit{T-Stick}~\cite{malloch2007tstick} senses touch and pressure along its shaft together
with accelerometer-derived shake and tap gestures. Conducting interfaces include Mathews'
\textit{Radio Baton}~\cite{mathews1991radiobaton}, Marrin and Paradiso's \textit{Digital
Baton}~\cite{marrin1997digitalbaton}, and the \textit{Conductor's
Jacket}~\cite{marrin1998conductorsjacket}. Nam's \textit{mPoi}~\cite{nam2013mpoi} made a swung
object into a DMI and builds its sound from the path and momentum of the swing. \textit{x-OSC}~\cite{madgwick2013xosc}
popularised the Wi-Fi/OSC inertial streaming pattern that REMORA's sensor unit follows.

\subsection{Martial Arts and Object Manipulation}
Takahata et al.~\cite{takahata2004karate} gave karate beginners wearable sound feedback while they
practised the basic punch, \emph{tsuki}, which is the same gesture our thrust detector targets. Their
goal was training rather than performance, and they found that the sound made practice more
enjoyable. Juggling is a closer cousin of staff work in one respect: it is a skilled object
practice with its own failure modes. \"Ozcan and \c{C}amc{\i}~\cite{ozcan2024juggling} use it to
make failure itself musical material. In our search of the NIME proceedings we did not find a
performance instrument built on a martial-arts weapon such as a staff, sword, or nunchaku. That
search was not exhaustive, and we would welcome pointers.

\subsection{Mapping Tools and Visual Patching}
Visual dataflow patching descends from Puckette's Patcher, now
\textit{Max}~\cite{puckette1988patcher,puckette2002maxatseventeen}, and continues in \textit{Pure
Data}~\cite{puckette1996puredata} and \textit{VCV Rack}~\cite{belt2017vcvrack}. For DMIs,
libmapper~\cite{malloch2015libmapper} and its graphical front end
Webmapper~\cite{wang2019webmapper} connect controller signals to synthesis parameters across a
network, and \textit{ossia score}~\cite{celerier2015ossia} combines such mappings with a timeline.
Any of these could host REMORA's mappings. We put the graph inside the plugin for practical
reasons: mapping and synthesis share one process and one preset file, and the patch shows each
signal's live value next to the synthesiser input it drives (\S\ref{sec:live-patching}). The T-Stick
with libmapper is the closest prior system. Where REMORA differs is the object. A full-length swung
staff has a spin plane, a spin direction, and a line it works along, and a handheld rod's touch and
shake gestures have nothing that corresponds to them.

\subsection{Mapping Strategies}
Hunt and Wanderley~\cite{hunt2002mapping} classify mappings as one-to-one, one-to-many, or
many-to-one, and argue that richer mappings make an interface feel more like an instrument. Van Nort
et al.~\cite{vannort2014mapping} extend mapping to continuous navigation between parameter spaces,
whereas REMORA's graph stays with explicit parameter-level functions.
\textit{Wekinator}~\cite{fiebrink2010wekinator} and Fran\c{c}oise et
al.~\cite{francoise2014probabilistic} learn mappings from example gestures, so the performer does
not have to wire them. Expressive-movement systems often describe motion with Laban's Effort
factors~\cite{camurri2000eyesweb,schacher2010motiontogesture}, and Fdili Alaoui et
al.~\cite{fdilialaoui2017laban} show that capturing those well takes expert annotation and
multimodal data. We deliberately stay with plain kinematic signals tied to the staff.

\section{Design}
Five requirements shaped the design:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A real b\^o.} The instrument keeps the reach, weight, and two-handed grip of a
full-length staff.
\item \textbf{Wireless during performance.} A cable is acceptable only for charging.
\item \textbf{Live-editable mapping.} Loading or rewiring a mapping must not interrupt audio.
\item \textbf{Mount-independent calibration.} The sound should not depend on how the enclosure is
rotated around the staff.
\item \textbf{Low latency.} The sound should follow the motion closely enough to feel driven by it
(\S\ref{sec:latency}).
\end{itemize}
The firmware estimates orientation and sends Open Sound Control (OSC) packets over Wi-Fi. The plugin
does everything after that: reception, calibration, motion analysis, graph evaluation, and
synthesis.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Hardware and Firmware}
\label{sec:build}
The sensor unit is a SparkFun ESP32-S2 Thing Plus reading an MPU-9250 inertial measurement unit
(IMU) over I2C at 400\,kHz, powered by a 2000\,mAh LiPo battery (Figure~\ref{fig:hardware}). It sits
in a parametric OpenSCAD enclosure sized to the staff (Figure~\ref{fig:enclosure}) and is zip-tied
near one end, outside the grip, so it can be moved or removed without damaging the staff. The
enclosure holds the board flat against the staff, so one board axis always runs along it. Which
axis that is, and how the board is rolled around the staff, is left to calibration. The position
along the staff does matter (\S\ref{sec:features}) and should stay the same between sessions.

The IMU is read at 200\,Hz with ranges of $\pm16$\,g and $\pm2000^\circ$/s; fast spins come close to
the gyroscope limit. Orientation is estimated on the microcontroller by a 6-axis Mahony
filter~\cite{mahony2008complementary}. The magnetometer is not fused, because next to the staff's
own electronics and stage equipment it is unreliable. Our first version used the fixed-gain filter
of the \texttt{hideakitai/MPU9250} library ($K_p = 30$), which trusts the accelerometer as a gravity
reference at all times. On a staff that is a poor assumption. With the unit about $r = 0.7$\,m from
the hands, a 2\,rev/s spin adds a centripetal acceleration of $\omega^2 r \approx 11$\,g. The
current filter scales $K_p$ by two weights: one that falls from 1 to 0 as $\bigl|\,|a| - 1\,\mathrm{g}\bigr|$
grows from 0.1 to 0.3\,g, and one that falls from 1 to 0 as the rotation rate grows from 90 to
180$^\circ$/s. During fast motion the filter therefore integrates the gyroscope alone and corrects
tilt once the staff slows down. The gyroscope bias is averaged at boot and then tracked whenever the
staff has been still for half a second. Heading has no absolute reference, so this tracking is the
only defence against yaw drift.

Each packet carries the quaternion, the raw acceleration, angular rate, and magnetic field, a
sequence number, and the sensor's clock in microseconds, and is sent over UDP every 10\,ms. The
host computer runs a Wi-Fi hotspot, the staff joins it, and packets go to the network's gateway,
which is the host. The plugin checks every argument's type, drops duplicate or out-of-order packets,
and counts lost ones from the sequence number. It shows inter-arrival time and network jitter (the
spread of arrival time minus sensor time) live, and it can log every packet to a file.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}
Orientation is a unit quaternion, and rotating $v$ by $q$ gives $v' = q\,v\,q^{-1}$. The performer
is prompted through three poses: $A$, staff horizontal pointing forward; $B$, staff vertical
pointing up; and $C$, staff horizontal pointing right. These give quaternions $q_A,q_B,q_C$.

\textbf{Stage 1: which board axis runs along the staff.} Each candidate
$L \in \{\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\}$ is scored by
\[
v_P = \widehat{\mathrm{rotate}(L, q_P)}\,,\qquad e(L) = |v_A{\cdot}v_B| + |v_B{\cdot}v_C| +
|v_C{\cdot}v_A|\,.
\]
Only the staff axis is carried through three mutually orthogonal directions by the three poses, so
it scores lowest. Because the search covers only the six board axes, it relies on the enclosure
holding one of them along the staff. $L$ and $-L$ score the same. The tip is the one that points
forward, up, and right in $A$, $B$, $C$, and physically forward $\times$ up $=$ right, so the tip's
triple product $(v_A \times v_B)\cdot v_C$ should be $+1$. In our frames it is $-1$, and the reason
is a convention we inherited. The library feeds its filter $(-a_x, a_y, a_z)$ and $(g_x, -g_y,
-g_z)$, which mirrors the chip's $x$ axis. The quaternion is therefore a proper rotation between two
mirror images of physical space. Every vector the plugin uses lives in that mirrored frame, so
everything stays consistent with everything else, but cross products change sign. Our filter keeps
the same input convention so that existing calibrations and presets stay valid. Hence
\[
L^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{L\,:\,(v_A \times v_B)\cdot v_C < -0.5} e(L)\,,
\]
which gives the alignment quaternion $q_{\text{align}}$. If no axis qualifies, or if
$e(L^\ast) > 1.5$ (roughly, some pair of poses more than $30^\circ$ from a right angle), calibration
is rejected and the performer is asked to try again.

\textbf{Stage 2: heading and tilt.} Let $b_0$ and $b_1$ be the measured staff direction in poses $A$
and $B$, and $n = \widehat{b_0 \times b_1}$. The triad $U = [\,b_0,\ n,\ (b_0 \times n)\,]$ is built
from them, and $W$ likewise from the instrument-frame forward $(1,0,0)$ and up $(0,0,1)$. The
rotation $R = W U^{\top}$ takes the measured directions onto the instrument's, and it is converted to
a quaternion $q_{\text{corr}}$.

\textbf{Stage 3: zero of roll.} Stages 1 and 2 fix where the staff points but leave its roll at
whatever angle the enclosure happens to sit around the staff. That matters for presets driven by
roll (\preset{Simple}, \preset{Spin Voices}). A last rotation $q_{\text{roll}}$ about the staff axis
makes pose $A$ read as zero roll. Every reading is then mapped to instrument space by
\[
q' = q_{\text{corr}}\; q_{\text{raw}}\; q_{\text{align}}\; q_{\text{roll}}\,,
\]
so roll is measured from how the performer held the staff in pose $A$, not from how the box was
strapped on. The angular rate and acceleration are rotated into the same frame. A ``re-zero
heading'' button reuses $q_{\text{corr}}$ to make the staff's current direction the new forward
without repeating the poses. The position of the unit along the staff is not corrected.

\subsection{Motion Features}
\label{sec:features}
One analyzer computes the features in Table~\ref{tab:motion-features}, and every mapping graph reads
from it. It runs once per packet, in order, with $\Delta t$ taken from the sensor's clock
(\S\ref{sec:fixes}). Smoothing rates and decays were tuned by ear with one update per 256-sample
block at 48\,kHz. They are now written per \emph{reference tick} of $T = 5.33$\,ms and converted to
the actual step, so that $\alpha' = 1-(1-\alpha)^{\Delta t/T}$ for a one-pole coefficient and
$d' = d^{\,\Delta t/T}$ for a decay.

\textbf{Tip, axis, and gravity.} The tip direction is $p = \mathrm{rotate}((1,0,0), q')$. The
rotation axis comes straight from the gyroscope, rotated into the world frame as $\omega$, with the
roll about the staff removed: $\omega_\perp = \omega - p\,(p\cdot\omega)$. Rolling the staff in the
hands is not a spin, and the lap count accumulates $\lVert\omega_\perp\rVert\,\Delta t$, not
$\lVert\omega\rVert\,\Delta t$. Because the specific force is also in the world frame, the dynamic
acceleration is simply $a_{\text{dyn}} = a_{\text{world}} - (0,0,1)$\,g.

\textbf{Energy features.} Four smoothed signals describe how hard the staff is moving.
\emph{Energy} is the enveloped magnitude of a leaky integral of $a_{\text{dyn}}$. \emph{Onset} is the
positive rate of change of rotation speed. \emph{Axis stability} is the smoothed absolute dot product
between consecutive rotation axes; it is high while the staff keeps turning about the same axis.
\emph{Jerk} is the smoothed jerk of $|a_{\text{dyn}}|$, given both as an ``abrupt'' value and as its
complement. During spins, centripetal acceleration dominates $a_{\text{dyn}}$, so Energy and Jerk
mostly follow spin speed. That is how the presets use them, and it is also why their scale changes
if the unit is moved along the staff.

\textbf{Spin plane and direction.} Let $(a_x,a_y,a_z)$ be $\omega_\perp$ after one-pole smoothing.
The spin is \emph{vertical} (the staff turns in a vertical plane, like a baton twirled beside the
body) when
\[
\sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2} > |a_z|\,,
\]
and \emph{horizontal} (like a propeller overhead) otherwise. For a horizontal spin, the direction is
the sign of $a_z$. For a vertical spin it is the sign of the larger of $a_x, a_y$. In the
\preset{Martial} presets it is instead the sign of the axis' dot product with a reference axis
latched at the first vertical spin after the preset loads.

\textbf{Facing.} A compass heading would need the magnetometer. Facing is instead a two-state
feature: is the staff working \emph{along} the calibration's forward line or \emph{across} it? It
does not tell front from back or left from right, and we no longer call the states ``north'' and
``east''. During a vertical spin, the spin plane decides. When the staff is not turning about the
vertical (rotation about $z$ below $60^\circ$/s) and is within $60^\circ$ of horizontal, the
direction it points decides. In every other case the state is held. That last rule is important. In
our first version, an overhead spin fell back on the tip direction, which sweeps through every
heading, so facing toggled every quarter turn. Let $(d_x,d_y)$ be the horizontal normal of the line
the staff works along: the smoothed rotation axis in a vertical spin, or the tip direction turned by
$90^\circ$ otherwise. With hysteresis factor $k = 1.15$, the state switches
\[
\text{across}\rightarrow\text{along if } |d_y| > k\,|d_x|\,,\qquad
\text{along}\rightarrow\text{across if } |d_x| > k\,|d_y|\,,
\]
that is, at $\arctan(1.15) \approx 49^\circ$ and $\arctan(1/1.15) \approx 41^\circ$. Facing is only as
good as the heading. Gyroscope bias is tracked (\S\ref{sec:build}) and heading can be re-zeroed, but
we have not yet measured how far it drifts over a set.

\textbf{Axial thrust (experimental).} A thrust is flagged when the jerk of $a_{\text{dyn}}\cdot p$
crosses zero while $|a_{\text{dyn}}\cdot p|$ exceeds $1.5$\,g and the staff is turning slower than
$90^\circ$/s, followed by a 0.2\,s cooldown. It failed in the pilot. The causes we found and fixed
are in \S\ref{sec:fixes}. It passes our simulated tests (\S\ref{sec:sim}), but we will not count it as
a contribution until it has been tested on labelled thrusts with the real staff.

\subsection{Mapping Architecture}
\label{sec:mapping-arch}
The node catalog has four categories: Source (sensor values and the features above), Math, Sink
(one node per synthesis engine exposing all its parameters as typed inputs, plus output gain), and
Display (live monitors). A graph is a set of nodes plus typed wires, saved as an XML preset. The
graph is the only path from sensor to synthesiser.

When a wire is added, the graph recomputes a topological order with Kahn's algorithm and refuses
the wire if it would close a cycle. For every packet, evaluation walks the cached order once, and
each node computes $y_i = f_{\text{type}(n_i)}(x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m};\theta_i)$ from its inputs $x$
and parameters $\theta$. The patch is thus one composed function of the sensor stream, with no
sorting on the audio thread. If the editor holds the graph when a packet arrives, evaluation does not
wait; the synthesiser keeps the previous values.

Math nodes cover arithmetic, shaping (linear and logarithmic range mapping, clamp, threshold,
crossfade, quantise, sine, atan2, semitones to Hz), lookup tables (1-D, and an 8-entry table indexed
by three booleans), and stateful dynamics (one-pole smoother, leaky integrator, retrigger envelope,
hysteresis, derivative, latch, random sample-and-hold). The stateful nodes use the same reference
tick as the analyzer, so a patch behaves the same at any buffer size.

The catalog has 69 node types (33 source, 29 math, 4 sink, 3 display). Presets range from 7 nodes
(\preset{Simple}) to 178 (\preset{Martial Effort}), with a median of 86. Together they use 50 of the
69 types, and most of the unused ones are raw per-axis sensor values. No preset needed a node type
of its own. That shows the catalog is enough for these mappings, but the catalog and the mappings
grew together, so it does not show that someone else's mappings would fit. Per-preset counts are
generated from the code by a tool in the repository. We checked the rebuilt presets against the
original classes by ear only. The fixes in \S\ref{sec:fixes} have since changed the features
underneath both, so an exact comparison with the old classes is no longer meaningful. Instead, any
two versions can now be compared by replaying the same packet log through them.

\textbf{Azimut root table.} The Azimut presets and the fast branch of \preset{Speed Gate} choose the
root note from three booleans (vertical spin, counter-clockwise, working along the forward line)
through the 8-entry lookup node. The target pitch is reached by a one-pole glide whose rate grows
with rotation speed and jumps at motion onset, so faster motion moves to the new root more quickly
(Figure~\ref{fig:editor}).

\textbf{Kinetic timbre.} \preset{Azimut Kinetic} keeps the Azimut root and maps one smoothed
rotation-energy value $E \in [0,1]$ (gyroscope magnitude normalised over 30--750$^\circ$/s) to many
timbre parameters at once~\cite{hunt2002mapping}: lowpass cutoff, partial amplitudes, drive, noise,
vibrato, tremolo, and reverb. A still staff sounds close to a pure sine; fast spins sound buzzy,
noisy, and reverberant. Each completed lap also triggers a random sample-and-hold, whose values
drift slowly into the same parameters, so two similar spins give two nearby but different timbres.
The whole preset is built from existing math nodes.

\subsection{Live Patching}
\label{sec:live-patching}
The node-graph editor (Figure~\ref{fig:editor}) is a pannable, zoomable canvas. Nodes are coloured
by category, wires are drawn by dragging between typed pins, and new nodes come from a searchable,
categorised picker. Hovering over any pin shows its current value, and Display nodes (number, meter,
scope) can be placed on a wire to monitor it. A changed patch can be kept or reverted to its saved
preset in one click.

Who patches, and when? A performer holding a 1.5\,m staff in both hands cannot rewire anything in
the middle of a kata, and a 100-node preset is not something to read at a glance. In practice the
editor is an authoring tool. Between takes in rehearsal, the player puts the staff down, changes a
range or swaps which feature drives a parameter, picks the staff up, and tries again, with audio
running the whole time. In performance the player switches between prepared presets. A second
person at the computer could edit while the player plays, but we have not tried that on stage.

\subsection{Synthesis}
\label{sec:synthesis}
Synthesis engines share one interface and are skipped entirely when the active preset does not wire
them. Every preset except \preset{Vocal Tract} drives the additive engine: four voices of six
harmonic partials, with soft-clip waveshaping, vibrato, tremolo, chorus, filtered noise, a
state-variable lowpass, and reverb. \preset{Vocal Tract} drives a C++ port (via
\texttt{cutelabnyc/pink-trombone-cpp}, MIT-licensed) of Neil Thapen's \textit{Pink
Trombone}~\cite{thapen2017pinktrombone}; only the control layer around it is ours. Engine parameters
are smoothed per sample, so values that change once per packet do not cause zipper noise. If no
packet arrives for 150\,ms, the master gain fades out over 120\,ms, and fades back in over 10\,ms when
packets return.

\subsection{How the Software Got Here}
We built the software in three steps. The first was a minimal OSC receiver with one hard-coded
tilt-to-pitch mapping. The second added mappings as separate C++ classes on top of a shared
analyzer, each one bringing new vocabulary (chords, energy features, facing, thrust). By the time
there were eleven of them, it was clear that they differed only in how they wired together the same
arithmetic, shaping, and stateful pieces. So the third step replaced the classes with the graph
engine. Since then a second synthesis engine (\S\ref{sec:synthesis}) and four new presets have been
added without touching the engine.

\section{Playing the B\^o: From Technique to Sound}
\label{sec:technique}
We designed the staff-specific features around families of b\^o technique, and this is where the
presets differ most from a generic motion controller.
% TODO (authors): ground this section in your own practice: name the style and the kata or
% drills you use, describe a short performance or étude, and say what playing a familiar form
% through REMORA felt like.

\textbf{Spins.} Two-handed spins beside the body and overhead spins are the staff's most
characteristic motions. Spin plane and direction select among four roots in
\preset{Martial Momentum}, so switching from a vertical clockwise spin to a horizontal one is heard
as a change of harmony. In the Azimut presets, the length of a spin, through the lap count, is
heard as a slow timbral sweep.

\textbf{Changes of direction.} A kata follows a floor pattern (\emph{embusen}) with turns between
facings. Facing is the third boolean of the Azimut root table, so the same spin played after a
quarter turn lands on a different root, and a form traced across the floor becomes a sequence of
harmonic regions. A half turn does not change the root, because facing only knows the line, not the
side. Whether that is a limitation or a feature depends on the form, and we have not settled it.

\textbf{Stances and transitions.} Held guard positions (\emph{kamae}) are almost static, so they
show up as tilt and roll. In \preset{Simple} and \preset{Bowed Chord}, the stance chooses the pitch
or chord. In \preset{Azimut Kinetic}, stillness gives a near-pure tone and energy brightens it, so
the contrast between a held stance and the move out of it shapes the timbre directly.

\textbf{Thrusts and strikes.} Thrusts (\emph{tsuki}) and strikes are short and decisive and mostly
run along the staff. They are what the axial detector targets, and they are what pilot participants
missed in the sound (\S\ref{sec:pilot}).

\subsection{Pilot Session}
\label{sec:pilot}
Ten adults with no prior exposure to REMORA took part in a short, informal pilot in August 2026 (see
Ethical Statement). It used the earlier firmware and analyzer, before the changes in
\S\ref{sec:fixes}. Each participant was calibrated, given a brief demonstration, and then played
freely across a subset of the nine selector presets before filling in an anonymous questionnaire.
The group does not match the intended performer. Nine of the ten rated their martial-arts or staff
experience 1 or 2 out of 5. General musical experience averaged 3.4/5, and prior experience with
gestural instruments 2.6/5. The results therefore say something about how newcomers meet the
instrument and little about how a trained staff player would use it.

We wrote the questionnaire for this pilot. It asked for agreement with six statements about the
playing experience on a five-point scale (Table~\ref{tab:pilot}), followed by preset preference and
free text. It is not a standard DMI evaluation instrument~\cite{wanderley2002evaluation,omodhrain2011framework},
and with N=10 the ratings are only descriptive. Comfort scored highest, followed by novelty and
absorption; predictability and expressiveness scored lower and varied more.

Preferences were spread out. \preset{Azimut Kinetic} was chosen most often (3/10), then
\preset{Wind Noise} and \preset{Lead+Drone} (2/10 each), and \preset{Spin Voices (Scale)} and

easiest to steer. The tenth participant named no single preset (``All of them are great'') and
pointed to live patching instead (``the ability to customize live and create new patches directly
inside with the node things is great''). Another asked for the wiring interface to be easier,
``maybe some kind of tutorial''.

Thrusts were the one complaint we had not anticipated. Three of ten participants said that thrusts
which felt physically decisive did nothing to the sound (``The thrust are not well detected'';
``Add `thrust' detection support or fix the existing''). Other frustrations were described as
learning (``when a bit hard it's just a learning experience''), and one participant found the
instrument fun but not, on its own, motivating to practise.

\subsection{What the Thrust Complaint Uncovered}
\label{sec:fixes}
Looking for the cause of the thrust problem, we found three defects. Each one affected more than
thrusts.

\textbf{Per-block analysis.} The analyzer ran once per audio block on whichever packet was newest,
with $\Delta t$ from the host clock. At 48\,kHz with 256-sample buffers, each 10\,ms packet was
analysed about twice. Every derivative-based feature saw one large step followed by a zero, its
size depended on the buffer size, and the thrust detector could see a false zero-crossing of jerk.
Packets are now queued from the network thread to the audio thread, and each one is analysed exactly
once, with $\Delta t$ from the sensor's clock. The mapping graph is evaluated once per packet too.

\textbf{Gravity in the wrong frame.} Dynamic acceleration was computed as the raw acceleration, in
the chip's frame, minus gravity rotated into the calibrated staff frame. Those two frames differ by
the board's alignment and by the mirrored axis described in \S\ref{sec:calibration}. A still,
tilted staff therefore showed up to 2\,g of ``motion'' (\S\ref{sec:sim}). The threshold for a thrust
is 1.5\,g, so the detector's input was largely a tilt signal. We now think this, more than
scheduling, is why thrusts went unheard. It also fed into Energy and Jerk. Both vectors are now
rotated into the world frame before they are subtracted.

\textbf{Tilt lost during spins.} With the library's fixed-gain filter, centripetal acceleration
pulled the estimated vertical towards the staff during fast spins, and spin plane, facing, and the
tip vector all inherit that error. The gated filter (\S\ref{sec:build}) and the gyroscope-based
rotation axis (\S\ref{sec:features}) address it.

The facing fix (\S\ref{sec:features}) belongs with these too. Energy and Jerk now have a different
scale at rest, so the presets that use them will need to be checked again by ear on the staff.

\subsection{Checks in Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}
Each of the fixes above can be checked without the staff. A tool in the repository runs the
plugin's own calibration and analyzer code, and the firmware's filter, on simulated motion: ideal
kinematics plus sensor noise (0.01\,g and $0.3^\circ$/s standard deviation), with the sensor 0.7\,m
from the hands. These checks show that the code does what we intended. They do not show that it
works on a real staff, where hands, staff flex, and sensor errors are messier. The main results:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Calibration.} We tested all 24 axis-aligned board orientations, at 8 roll angles each
(192 mounts), with a random filter heading and 50 random staff orientations per mount. The worst tip
error was $0.03^\circ$. The same staff orientation read the same roll on every mount. With every pose
up to $10^\circ$ off, no calibration was rejected, but the tip error grew to $18^\circ$, so careful
poses matter.
\item \textbf{Filter during spins.} The worst tilt error during a 4\,s spin was as follows. For a
vertical-plane spin at 1\,rev/s: $179^\circ$ with fixed gain, $1.8^\circ$ gated. At 2\,rev/s:
$179^\circ$ against $3.6^\circ$. For an overhead spin at 2\,rev/s: $81^\circ$ against $0.0^\circ$.
With fixed gain the vertical is simply lost. Both filters recover once the staff is still.
\item \textbf{Gravity removal.} On a still staff, over 24 mounts and 20 orientations each, the
previous formula left a residual of 1.29\,g on average and 2.00\,g at worst. The current one leaves
none.
\item \textbf{Scheduling.} We simulated twenty thrusts of 2--4\,g at random headings and tilts, then
20\,s of spins and swings as negatives. Per-packet analysis found 20/20 thrusts with no false
detections, with identical output at buffer sizes of 64 to 1024 samples. Scheduled per block, with
the gravity fix already in place, the same analyzer also found all 20. Its onset peak, however,
varied from 0.27 to 0.55 with buffer size, against a constant 0.26 per packet.
\item \textbf{Facing.} Over a 5\,s overhead spin at 2\,rev/s, facing changed 0 times; the previous
rule changed it 40 times. A quarter turn of the body changed it exactly once, both when the staff
was held still and when it went from a side spin to a spin in the other plane.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Latency}
\label{sec:latency}
We have not measured end-to-end latency. It can be broken down into parts, two of which are
unknown. The sample can be up to 5\,ms old (200\,Hz IMU), and it may wait up to 10\,ms for the next
send. Wi-Fi delivery comes next, and it is not yet measured. Once a packet arrives, it waits up to one
block (5.3\,ms at 256 samples and 48\,kHz) to be analysed, and the result then goes through the
host's output buffer, another 5.3\,ms plus whatever the audio device adds. Without the network and
the device, that is 5--26\,ms. The figure of about 50\,ms in an earlier draft was a guess, and we
withdraw it. Most presets map continuous motion to gliding parameters, which tolerate delay better
than percussive events do. Thrusts are the exception, because their sound should land with the
impact, and even the known part of the budget can exceed the 10\,ms often cited for intimate
control~\cite{wessel2002intimate}. The plugin's packet log and live jitter display make the network
part easy to measure, and a contact microphone on the staff recorded alongside the plugin's output
will give the total.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\textbf{Evidence.} Every fix in \S\ref{sec:fixes} has so far been checked only in simulation, and the
only user data predates the fixes and comes mostly from novices.

\textbf{Heading.} Facing depends on a heading with no absolute reference. Bias tracking slows drift
and the re-zero button resets it, but the button needs a hand free, and we have no gesture for it
yet.

\textbf{Sensor position.} Calibration corrects rotation but not position along the staff, so the
acceleration-based features depend on where the unit is mounted.

\textbf{Network.} The hotspot name and password are compiled into the firmware, so pairing with a
different host means reflashing over USB. A laptop hotspot limits range and is sensitive to
interference. There is no host discovery beyond sending to the gateway, and the only feedback when
joining fails is a blinking LED.

\textbf{Interface.} The graph editor serves patch authors better than performers who only want to
choose a preset. There is no simplified performance view.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
The immediate work is the measurement list in \S\ref{sec:not-measured}, together with re-tuning the
presets by ear on the corrected features. Replacing the hotspot with ESP-NOW, sending to a second
ESP32 on the host's USB port, would take network credentials out of the firmware. Longer term, we
plan a dual-IMU configuration, one sensor at each end of the staff, which would also let centripetal
acceleration be separated from linear motion. We also plan gesture-triggered preset switching and a
heading re-zero gesture, and a shareable patch format.

\section{Conclusions}
REMORA makes a b\^o into a musical instrument whose features (spin plane, spin direction, the line
the staff works along, lap count) come from the way a staff is handled. Its mapping is a patch that
can be edited while audio runs, and all of the project's hand-coded mappings now live as presets of
one node catalog. First-time players in a ten-person pilot found it comfortable and engaging and
most often preferred a spin- and facing-driven preset. Their one clear complaint, that thrusts were
not heard, led us to three defects that a stock orientation filter and a per-block analyzer
introduce on a long spinning object. We fixed them and checked the fixes in simulation. What remains
is to show the same on the real staff, with trained players.

\section{Ethical Statement}
\textbf{Accessibility.} REMORA assumes the mobility and two-handed grip strength needed to swing a
1--1.5\,kg staff, and no reduced-effort physical variant exists. The skill barrier varies across

for a newcomer, but this does not address the physical requirement.

battery can be removed and reused. No lifecycle analysis has been performed.

answered an anonymous questionnaire. No names or identifying data were recorded, free-text answers
are quoted without attribution, and participants could stop at any time. Each participant gave
