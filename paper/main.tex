\begin{abstract}
REMORA turns a martial arts staff (b\^o) into a musical instrument: spins, tilts, and strikes become
sound in real time. A small wireless sensor unit on the staff tracks its motion and sends it to a
companion audio plugin, which we built around a simple idea - instead of hard-wiring one fixed
gesture-to-sound behaviour, the mapping itself is a small patch of connected building blocks that
the performer can see and rewire live, on a visual canvas inside the plugin, much like a modular
synthesiser. We show that this same small set of building blocks is enough to reproduce, as editable
patches, everything from simple tilt-controlled pitch to compound movement-quality mappings inspired
by Laban Movement Analysis. This paper describes the instrument's hardware, its self-calibration
method, the mathematics behind turning raw orientation into musical control, and the live patching
interface itself, and reflects on what it means to make an instrument's gestural vocabulary an
explicit, editable part of the instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection*{Motivation and Background}
\textit{The b\^o staff is a full-body controller form factor that's had surprisingly little
attention in NIME - most inertial-sensor DMIs mount their sensor on the body or a small handheld
object, not a swung, two-handed staff with its own rotational inertia and reach. That gap is my
opening case. The claim I want on the table early: REMORA's contribution isn't one more fixed
gesture-to-sound mapping, but a reusable, block-rate visual programming layer embedded inside the
instrument itself - every named mapping described later in the paper is really just one saved patch
of that layer.}

\subsection*{Objectives of the Study}
\textit{Four things here feel genuinely new to me, and I still need to turn this list into proper
prose: (1) a small dataflow language (source / math / sink node primitives) embedded directly in the
audio plugin, evaluated as a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph) once per audio block, that a performer can
rewire live instead of me recompiling C++; (2) by construction, this same handful of primitives
turns out to be expressive enough to reproduce eleven previously hand-coded mapping strategies as
ordinary presets - the mapping design space I explored by hand over the course of this project is,
it turns out, spanned by a fairly compact algebra; (3) a magnetometer-free world-frame heading
estimate (``facing'') derived purely from the calibrated orientation stream, which sidesteps the
magnetic interference a stage rig would otherwise cause; (4) a mount-agnostic calibration transform,
so the same instrument logic runs no matter where the sensor physically sits on the staff.}

\subsection*{Scope and Applications}
\textit{REMORA targets solo live performance with a single staff and a single IMU - not an
installation piece, not a multi-performer controller (at least not yet, see Future Improvements).
Worth being upfront about what's out of scope for this paper too: no formal user study, no
controlled comparison against other staff instruments, and no claim that patching a mapping is
easier to learn than a fixed one - only that the capability exists and is expressive.}

\subsubsection*{Orientation and Calibration}

\textbf{Rotation.} Orientation is tracked as a unit quaternion rather than Euler angles, since
quaternions don't suffer from gimbal lock. Rotating a vector $v$ by orientation $q$ gives
\[
v' = q\,v\,q^{-1}
\]
where $q^{-1}$ is the conjugate of $q$. Euler angles (pitch/roll/yaw) are only computed once, right
at the end, for the handful of mappings that still need them.
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::rotate}}
\coderef{\texttt{rotateVectorByQuaternion}}
\coderef{\texttt{toEuler}}
\coderef{\texttt{multiply}}
\coderef{\texttt{conjugate}}

\textbf{Calibration, stage 1 - which axis is ``forward''.} The sensor chip can be glued onto the
staff at any orientation, so REMORA can't just assume which of its axes points along the staff.
During calibration the performer holds the staff in three reference poses, labelled $A, B$ and $C$,
giving three corresponding recorded quaternions $q_A, q_B, q_C$. For each of the six candidate axes
$L \in \{\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\}$, we rotate $L$ through all three poses and see how spread out the
results are:
\begin{align*}
v_P &= \widehat{\mathrm{rotate}(L, q_P)}\,,\quad P \in \{A,B,C\} \\
e(L) &= |v_A{\cdot}v_B| + |v_B{\cdot}v_C| + |v_C{\cdot}v_A|
\end{align*}
A genuine forward axis should point in three noticeably different directions across the three poses,
so a low $e(L)$ (near-orthogonal directions) is a good sign. The candidate with the lowest $e(L)$ -
as long as it also forms a right-handed triad, $(v_A \times v_B)\cdot v_C < 0$ - is kept as the
alignment quaternion $q_{\text{align}}$.
\coderef{\texttt{computeAlignQuat}}

\textbf{Calibration, stage 2 - fixing the remaining twist.} Picking the right axis isn't quite
enough, since the sensor could still be mounted rotated around it. So the three poses also give a
measured forward/up/right triad $b_f, b_u, b_r$, which is compared against where those directions
are \emph{supposed} to point in the ideal instrument frame, $r_f, r_u, r_r$. Both triads are first
straightened into right-angled axes with Gram-Schmidt:
\begin{align*}
e_0 &= b_f, & e_1 &= \widehat{b_f \times b_u}, & e_2 &= e_0 \times e_1 \\
f_0 &= r_f, & f_1 &= \widehat{r_f \times r_u}, & f_2 &= f_0 \times f_1
\end{align*}
and the rotation that best lines one triad up with the other has a simple closed form,
\[
R = F E^{\top}\,,\qquad F = [\,f_0\;\;f_1\;\;f_2\,]\,,\quad E = [\,e_0\;\;e_1\;\;e_2\,]
\]
$R$ is then converted to a quaternion $q_{\text{corr}}$ using Shepperd's method.
\coderef{\texttt{computeCorrection}}
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::fromMatrix}}

\textbf{Calibration, runtime application.} From then on, every live reading is converted into
instrument space with
\[
q' = q_{\text{corr}} \, \big(q_{\text{raw}} \, q_{\text{align}}\big)
\]
where $q_{\text{raw}}$ is what the sensor reports and $q_{\text{align}}$, $q_{\text{corr}}$ are the
two quaternions found above. The result doesn't depend on where on the staff the sensor happens to
be mounted.
\coderef{\texttt{REMORAProcessor::getCalibratedQuat}}

\subsubsection*{Motion Feature Extraction}
\textit{Everything below runs once per received packet, inside the shared analyzer that every
mapping graph reads from - so no individual mapping repeats this work.}
\coderef{\texttt{StaffMotionAnalyzer::computeFrame}}

\textbf{Timestep.} $\Delta t$ is the time between two packets. If it looks suspicious (outside
$0.1$-$200\,\text{ms}$), REMORA just reuses the last valid $\Delta t$ instead of trusting it - so a
dropped or duplicated packet can't send any of the integrators below flying.
\coderef{\texttt{calculateDeltaTime}}

\textbf{Tip vector and rotation axis.} The direction the staff's tip is pointing, $p_n =
\mathrm{rotate}((1,0,0), q_n)$, is kept in a rolling buffer of the last three samples. From those
three points we get the tip's velocity and, from that, the axis it's currently spinning around:
\[
\bar v = \tfrac{1}{2}(p_0 - p_2) \qquad\qquad \mathrm{axis} = p_1 \times \bar v
\]
where $p_0, p_1, p_2$ are the newest, middle, and oldest of the three buffered points.
\coderef{\texttt{updateTipPositionHistory}}
\coderef{\texttt{calculateRotationAxisAtMidpoint}}

\textbf{Gravity and dynamic acceleration.} The accelerometer always mixes gravity in with whatever
motion the performer is making. To separate the two, REMORA keeps a running estimate of which way
``down'' currently is, by smoothing the inverse-rotated world $+Z$ axis:
\[
g_n = \mathrm{onePole}\big(g_{n-1},\ \mathrm{rotate}(+Z,\, q_n^{-1}),\ \alpha{=}0.10\big)
\]
Subtracting this gravity estimate from the raw reading leaves just the motion: $a_{\text{dyn}} =
a_{\text{raw}} - g$.
\coderef{\texttt{updateGravityVector}}
\coderef{\texttt{calculateDynamicAcceleration}}

\textbf{One-pole smoothing.} One simple filter shows up everywhere below, so it's worth naming just
once:
\[
y_n = y_{n-1} + \alpha \,(x_n - y_{n-1})
\]
Each new output nudges partway from the previous output $y_{n-1}$ toward the new input $x_n$;
$\alpha \in (0,1]$ sets how big that step is - higher means it reacts faster, but gets noisier.
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::applyOnePoleFilter}}

\textbf{Laban Weight.} Meant to capture how forceful a movement feels. Dynamic acceleration is
integrated into a running velocity estimate, with old velocity constantly leaking away (an 80\,ms
half-life, so a staff left resting doesn't drift):
\[
v_n = \big(v_{n-1} + a_{\text{dyn}}\cdot 9.81 \cdot \Delta t\big)\cdot e^{-\Delta t \cdot 1000 \ln 2
/ 80}
\]
($9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$ just converts $a_{\text{dyn}}$ from g-units to physical units.) Weight is
$\lVert v_n \rVert$, scaled and clamped, pushed through an envelope that snaps up fast on impact
($\alpha_{\text{attack}}{=}0.40$) but settles back down slowly ($\alpha_{\text{release}}{=}0.006$).
\coderef{\texttt{integrateVelocityForLabanWeight}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanWeight}}

\textbf{Laban Time (suddenness).} Tracks how abruptly the rotation speed is changing. It's the rate
of change of the \emph{raw} gyroscope magnitude - deliberately not the smoothed version used
elsewhere, so a sharp onset isn't blurred out before it's measured:
\[
\dot{\lVert\omega\rVert} = \frac{\lVert\omega\rVert_n - \lVert\omega\rVert_{n-1}}{\Delta t}
\]
where $\omega$ is the gyroscope's angular-velocity vector (in $^\circ/\text{s}$). Only increases
count (it's clamped to its positive part), normalised by $800^\circ/\text{s}^2$, then given its own
fast-attack/slow-release envelope.
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanTime}}

\textbf{Laban Space (focus).} Captures how consistently the staff spins around the \emph{same} axis,
versus wandering. It's the absolute dot product between consecutive, normalised rotation-axis
directions, one-pole smoothed ($\alpha{=}0.10$) and relaxing back toward $0.5$ whenever the staff is
nearly still.
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanSpace}}

\textbf{Laban Flow.} Separates bound, controlled movement from free, flung movement, using the jerk
(rate of change) of the dynamic-acceleration magnitude:
\begin{align*}
\dot{\lVert a_{\text{dyn}}\rVert} &= \frac{\lVert a_{\text{dyn}}\rVert_n - \lVert
a_{\text{dyn}}\rVert_{n-1}}{\Delta t + 10^{-6}} \\
j_{\text{flow}} &= \frac{\big|\dot{\lVert a_{\text{dyn}}\rVert}\big|}{50}
\end{align*}
(the $10^{-6}$ just guards against dividing by zero). $j_{\text{flow}}$ is clamped to $[0,1]$ and
smoothed ($\alpha{=}0.12$) into \texttt{flow\_bound}; \texttt{flow\_free} is simply $1 -
\texttt{flow\_bound}$.
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanFlow}}

\textbf{Spin classification.} Is the staff spinning like a baton (vertical) or more like a fan blade
(horizontal)? The smoothed rotation axis ($\alpha{=}0.35$) is called vertical when its
horizontal-plane component outweighs its $z$ component. Spin direction (CW/CCW) then comes either
from the sign of the dominant in-plane axis component, or - in the Bozendo mappings - from the sign
of that axis's dot product against a reference heading latched once, the first time a qualifying
vertical spin happens after the mapping loads.
\coderef{\texttt{updateSpinClassificationByAbsoluteComponent}}
\coderef{\texttt{ByReferenceAzimuth}}

\textbf{Facing.} Rather than compute a compass heading - which would need a magnetometer, and stages
are full of magnetic interference - REMORA asks a simpler question: is the staff pointing more
east-west or more north-south? That's just a hysteresis comparison,
\[
|axis_x| \ \gtrless\ 1.15\cdot|axis_y|
\]
using whichever axis is currently relevant - the rotation axis during vertical spins, the tip
direction during horizontal ones. The $1.15$ factor gives asymmetric switch points
($\approx\!48^\circ/42^\circ$) so the classification doesn't flicker right at the boundary.
\coderef{\texttt{updateFacingClassification}}

\textbf{Continuous spin count.} Counts how many full turns the staff has made in its current spin,
regardless of direction. Degrees accumulate as $\lVert\omega\rVert \cdot \Delta t$; every
$360^\circ$ crossed resets the accumulator and bumps the lap counter by one. The counter itself
resets to $0$ the instant the spin classification changes.
\coderef{\texttt{accumulateContinuousSpins}}

\textbf{Axial thrust detection.} Catches a stabbing or striking motion along the staff's own axis.
Dynamic acceleration is rotated into world frame and projected onto the tip direction, giving a
signed axial acceleration $a_{\text{ax}}$. A thrust is flagged when its jerk
\[
j_{\text{ax}} = \frac{a_{\text{ax},n} - a_{\text{ax},n-1}}{\Delta t}
\]
crosses zero right as $|a_{\text{ax}}|$ passes $1.5\text{g}$ - gated to $\lVert\omega\rVert <
90^\circ/\text{s}$ so a fast spin can't be mistaken for a stab, plus a 200\,ms cooldown between
hits. A detected hit sets the thrust envelope's target to $\mathrm{clamp}(\text{strength}+0.5,0,1)$,
and a $\times 0.94$-per-block decay ($\approx\!150$\,ms half-life) applies from then on, every
block, including the trigger block itself - so what actually shows up on a hit is $0.94\times$ that
target.
\coderef{\texttt{detectAxialThrustPeaks}}

\subsection*{Software: Mapping Architecture}
\textit{Here I explain the \texttt{NodeGraph}/\texttt{NodeTypeRegistry} design: three node
categories (Source - raw IMU channels and derived motion/Laban features; Math - arithmetic, shaping,
dynamics, lookup tables; Sink - synth parameters, from scalars like root frequency to indexed arrays
like per-voice gain/pan), each a small typed function registered once. A graph is just nodes plus
typed input-to-output wiring, serialised to/from XML (Extensible Markup Language) as a preset - and
I want to be clear that \texttt{GraphMappingStrategy} makes the graph itself the only mapping type
the synth ever talks to; there's no separate hand-written mapping path anymore.}

\subsubsection*{Graph Evaluation and Primitive Library}

\textbf{DAG evaluation.} A patch is a directed graph of typed nodes; \texttt{NodeGraph::connect}
provisionally adds an edge and recomputes a topological order with Kahn's algorithm (in-degree
queue) via \texttt{NodeGraph::recomputeTopoOrder} - if the graph does not fully drain (a node's
in-degree never reaches zero), the edge closed a cycle and is reverted. Each audio block,
\texttt{NodeGraph::evaluate} walks the cached order once, and every node computes
\[
y_i = f_{\text{type}(n_i)}\big(x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}\,;\,\theta_i\big)
\]
where $n_i$ is the $i$-th node in topological order, $\text{type}(n_i)$ its registered node-type
function, $x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}$ its wired input values, $\theta_i$ its parameters, and $y_i$ its
resulting output(s) - so the whole patch is one composed function of the raw sensor frame, evaluated
once per block with no re-sorting on the audio thread.

\textbf{Primitive vocabulary.} Every node type is a single small pure function (or, for the few
stateful ones, a function plus one scratch struct), registered once: arithmetic (add / subtract /
multiply / divide / abs / max / min / equals), shaping (mapRange, clamp, threshold, crossfade,
quantizeSteps, sine, atan2, semitonesToHz), lookup tables (a general 1-D table and a
3-boolean-selector table - the same node type that implements the Azimut root table below), and
stateful dynamics (variable-rate one-pole smoother, leaky integrator, retrigger envelope, hysteresis
step, derivative, latched smoother).
\coderef{\texttt{Graph::buildAllNodes}}

\subsection*{Software: Named Mappings as Graph Presets}
\label{sec:azimut-deep-dive}
\textit{I'd rather show the generalisation claim than just assert it, so this subsection walks
through how Simple (tilt-to-pitch), Bowed Chord, Lead+Drone, Spin Filter, the Bozendo V1/V2
Laban-driven mappings, and the Azimut family (facing-based eight-way root table, spin-count filter

\subsubsection*{Azimut-Specific Transformations}
\textit{Shared by every Azimut-family preset.}
\coderef{\texttt{Graph::Presets::buildAzimutCore}}

\textbf{Root-note table.} The spin-plane/direction/facing root-note table above is not an if/else
chain - it is literally an 8-entry lookup table indexed by three booleans (\texttt{isVertical},
\texttt{isCCW}, \texttt{isFacingEast}) through the graph's generic 3-selector LUT node.

\textbf{Root morph.} The target semitone value is chased by a variable-rate one-pole filter whose
rate is
\[
\text{rate} = \max\Big(\mathrm{clamp}\big(\tfrac{\lVert\omega\rVert}{2000},\,0.005,\,0.2\big)\,,\ \
0.5\cdot\text{onsetEnv}\Big)
\]
where \texttt{onsetEnv} is a retrigger envelope (decay $0.90$) that fires the instant the staff
leaves rest - faster motion, or a fresh onset, morphs the root pitch in quicker.

\textbf{Filter sweep.} The spin count drives a phase modulator whose sine output is remapped onto
the filter's cutoff range and smoothed:
\begin{align*}
c_{\text{raw}} &=
\mathrm{mapRange}\big(\sin(1.5\cdot\text{spinCount})\,,\,[-1,1]\,,\,[400,20000]\text{Hz}\big) \\
\text{cutoff} &= \mathrm{onePole}\big(c_{\text{raw}}\,,\ \text{rate}{=}0.03\big)
\end{align*}
where $\text{spinCount}$ is the unsigned lap counter from Continuous Spin Count above,
$\mathrm{mapRange}$ linearly rescales its argument from the first interval to the second,
$c_{\text{raw}}$ the resulting unsmoothed cutoff target, and $\mathrm{onePole}$ the smoothing
primitive defined earlier.
\coderef{\texttt{buildSpinCountLpfHz}}

\subsection*{Graphical Interface: Live Patching as Performance Practice}
\textit{This is probably the subsection I want to spend the most space on after the mathematics.} I
want to present the node-graph editor as the instrument's primary authoring surface, not a debug
tool: a pannable, zoomable canvas on which each node renders as a coloured card - green for Source,
yellow for Sink, neutral for Math - labelled with its category and inline, live-editable parameter
fields; typed input/output pins along each card's edges accept click-and-drag connections rendered
as cubic-B\'ezier wires, colour-highlighted while dragging and hit-testable afterward for rewiring
or deletion via context menu. New nodes are added from a categorised picker at any canvas position;
an auto-layout pass untangles a freshly loaded preset into left-to-right source-to-sink columns. A
dirty-state indicator tracks whether the active patch has diverged from its saved preset, with
one-click revert - so a performer can improvise a mapping change mid-rehearsal and either keep or
discard it, treating the mapping itself as something authored and iterated on at the same level as
the sound it produces.

\subsection*{Software: Synthesis Engine}
\textit{Still need to write up \texttt{BoStaffSynth} here - the shared four-voice additive engine
and its mapping-agnostic parameter surface (gain, drive, noise, filter, pan, reverb send) that every
graph's sink nodes write into.}

\subsubsection*{Additive Synthesis Engine} \
\coderef{\texttt{BoStaffSynth::processBlock}}
\coderef{\texttt{SynthVoice::tick}}

\textbf{Harmonic bank.} Each of the 4 voices sums 6 phase-accumulated sine partials at integer
multiples of a (vibrato- and chorus-modulated) fundamental, each with its own independently smoothed
target amplitude.

\textbf{Waveshaping.} A rational soft-clip saturator, applied per-voice when \texttt{driveAmt} $>0$:
\[
y = \frac{x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})}{1+\big|x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})\big|}
\]
where $x$ is the voice's summed harmonic-bank sample before shaping and \texttt{driveAmt} the
mapping-supplied drive amount.

\textbf{Resonator.} A short feedback comb (1800-sample delay, $0.35$ feedback, mixed $0.7$ dry /
$0.3$ wet) is layered onto every voice for a string-like coloration.

\textbf{Modulation.} Vibrato and tremolo are sinusoidal multipliers on pitch and gain respectively:
\[
f' = f\,\big(1+\text{depth}\cdot\sin\phi_v\big)
\qquad\qquad
g' = g\,\big(1-\text{depth}\cdot(0.5+0.5\sin\phi_t)\big)
\]
where $f, g$ are the fundamental frequency and master gain before modulation, $f', g'$ the modulated
values actually used, and $\phi_v, \phi_t$ the vibrato and tremolo LFO phase accumulators (radians),
each advancing by its own rate every sample. A per-voice phase-offset sine (depth $0.003$, rate
$0.3$\,Hz) adds chorus detuning; chord changes cross-fade linearly between old and new semitone
offsets over $200$\,ms rather than jumping.

\textbf{Noise and filtering.} An xorshift32 PRNG feeds a one-pole lowpass (coefficient
\texttt{noiseLpCoef}) before being mixed in at \texttt{noiseAmount}; the summed stereo output passes
through a JUCE state-variable TPT lowpass, its cutoff set directly each block from the graph's
\texttt{sink.lpfCutoffHz} (only range-clamped, not ramped), and an optional Schroeder-style
\texttt{juce::dsp::Reverb} send whose wet level \emph{is} ramped through a per-sample
\texttt{SmoothedValue} to avoid zippering when the active mapping changes.

\textbf{Pitch/frequency conversion.}
\[
f = f_{\text{root}} \cdot 2^{\,\text{semitones}/12}
\]
where $f_{\text{root}}$ is the mapping's current root frequency in Hz and $\text{semitones}$ the
signed offset applied to it, giving the equal-tempered result $f$.
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::semitoneRatio}}
\coderef{\texttt{convertSemitonesToHertz}}

\section{Development Iterations}
\textit{Modelled on the reference paper's iteration log rather than presenting the final

\subsection*{Advantages and Limitations}
\textit{I want to be honest here about what the generalisation into a graph actually buys versus
what it costs: expressiveness and shareability of mappings, weighed against the added cognitive step
of patching for performers used to fixed instruments. Worth folding in the informal playtesting
observations, and I should be upfront about the current limitations - single-IMU ambiguity, no
formal user study yet, no perceptual evaluation of the facing/calibration accuracy.}

\subsection*{Future Improvements}
\textit{Extensions I keep coming back to: gesture-triggered mode switching, a merged rest/motion
mapping, a two-IMU dual-tip configuration, further Laban Effort mapping targets, ML-based discrete
gesture recognition, and eventually exposing the node graph itself as a shareable/community patch
format.}

\section{Conclusions}
\textit{I want to close by making REMORA's real novelty explicit: it's architectural, not just one
more instrument - collapsing eleven hand-built gesture mappings into presets of one small,
live-patchable dataflow language embedded in the plugin, grounded in an explicit mathematical
account of calibration, orientation, and movement-quality extraction.}
