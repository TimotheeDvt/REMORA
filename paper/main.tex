\begin{abstract}
REMORA turns a martial arts staff (b\^o) into a musical instrument: spins, tilts, and strikes become
sound in real time. A wireless sensor unit on the staff tracks its motion and sends it to a
companion audio plugin, which we built around a simple idea - instead of hard-wiring one fixed
gesture-to-sound behaviour, the mapping itself is a small patch of connected building blocks that
the performer can see and rewire live, on a visual canvas inside the plugin, much like a modular
synthesiser. We show that this same small set of building blocks is enough to reproduce, as editable
patches, everything from simple tilt-controlled pitch to compound movement-quality mappings inspired
by Laban Movement Analysis. This paper describes the instrument's hardware, its self-calibration
method, the mathematics behind turning raw orientation into musical control, and the live patching
interface itself. We situate it against prior staff-shaped, baton, and visual-patching instruments.
Grounded in implementation-level evidence, together with a protocol for the performer evaluation
still to be run, we reflect on what it means to make an instrument's gestural vocabulary an
explicit, editable part of the instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and Background}
REMORA grew out of the first author's parallel practice in martial arts and music, and a frustration
shared by many hand-built digital musical instruments: once a gesture vocabulary is designed and
coded, changing it means rebuilding the instrument rather than performing with it. That friction is
the paper's real starting point - not which single mapping best suits a staff, but whether a staff
instrument's gestural vocabulary could instead be something the performer authors and revises live,
the way a modular-synthesis patch is built and rebuilt on a rack.

The b\^o staff itself is a form factor that New Interfaces for Musical Expression (NIME) instruments
have rarely explored: a full-body, swung, two-handed controller with its own rotational inertia
and reach, distinct from the small handheld or body-mounted objects most inertial-sensor Digital
Musical Instruments (DMIs) use. Pairing that distinctive controller form with a
mapping layer that can be reshaped at performance time, rather than fixed at design time, is the
combination this paper investigates.

\subsection{Objectives of the Study}
This article supports four claims, each grounded in the system as it currently operates:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{claim:dag} A small dataflow language - source, math, and sink node primitives - is embedded directly in
the audio plugin and evaluated once per audio block as a directed acyclic graph (DAG); the performer
rewires it live, without recompiling any code.
\item \label{claim:presets} That same handful of primitives is expressive enough to reproduce eleven previously hand-coded
mapping strategies as ordinary presets: the mapping design space explored by hand over the course of
the project turns out to be spanned by a fairly compact algebra of building blocks.
\item \label{claim:facing} A magnetometer-free, world-frame heading estimate (``facing'') is derived purely from the
calibrated orientation stream, eluding the magnetic interference a stage rig would otherwise cause -
a claim we bound to session lengths on the order of tens of minutes, since uncorrected gyroscopic
yaw drift is expected to accumulate over longer, uninterrupted play (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
\item \label{claim:calibration} A mount-agnostic calibration transform lets the same instrument logic run correctly regardless
of where the sensor is physically mounted on the staff.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Applications}
The instrument's intended context follows directly from its origin: a solo performer with some
staff or martial-arts background, playing a set built from one or more presets rather than a fixed,
single-piece score. The preset catalog (Table~\ref{tab:preset-nodes}) is deliberately graded rather than uniform - \preset{Simple}
asks only for basic tilt and roll, while the Laban-driven \preset{Martial} and \preset{Azimut} presets reward
staff-handling vocabulary the performer already has from bo practice - so the same instrument can
serve a newcomer exploring gesture-to-sound relationships and a martial artist bringing an existing
physical vocabulary onto a stage. Because the mapping itself is authored in the same visual
language a modular-synthesis performer would recognise, we also see REMORA as being of interest to
patch-based electronic musicians looking for a full-body controller to wire into an existing
practice, not only to staff practitioners looking for a way into live electronics. We do not,
however, claim REMORA is suited to ensemble or accompanied performance in its current form: the
single-IMU, single-staff design in \S\ref{sec:limitations} means the instrument has no notion of a
second performer's motion, and no mapping in the current catalog was designed with an ensemble
context in mind.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Staff, Rod, and Baton DMIs}
Rod-, staff-, and baton-shaped controllers form a recurring family of DMIs, spanning handheld
sensor-augmented rods, conducting tools, and swung objects.

\textit{T-Stick}~\cite{malloch2007tstick} - a family of rod-shaped controllers sensing finger
position and pressure along the shaft via capacitive touch, together with accelerometer-derived
shake and tap gestures. The sensing is paired with an external, reconfigurable mapping layer built
around the digital-orchestra/libmapper tools, so the gesture vocabulary can be revised without
changing the hardware.

\textit{Digital Baton}~\cite{marrin1997digitalbaton} - Marrin and Paradiso's controller, combining
accelerometers, infrared position tracking, and a pressure-sensitive tip to capture continuous
conducting gesture rather than discrete note events.

\textit{Conductor's Jacket}~\cite{marrin1998conductorsjacket} - a wearable, full-body
electromyography (EMG) and motion rig extending the same conducting-gesture goal to the whole body,
recording muscle tension and movement alongside the baton's hand-level detail.

\textit{Musical Poi (mPoi)}~\cite{nam2013mpoi} - Nam's sensor-augmented poi, generating sound from
swing motion; a compact, single-mapping instrument that established spun/swung objects as a
recognised, if uncommon, DMI form.

\subsection{Inertial Sensing and Visual Patching in NIME}
Visual dataflow patching and Laban-derived movement analysis are two long-running, separate
threads in NIME and related work: one concerned with how a mapping is authored and rewired, the
other with what expressive qualities a mapping can draw on.

\textit{Max}~\cite{puckette1988patcher} - originated as Puckette's Patcher and has been maintained
and extended commercially for over three decades, a history Puckette himself traces in
``Max at Seventeen''~\cite{puckette2002maxatseventeen}. It established the boxes-and-patch-cords
model of unit generators wired together and rewired live that much of visual audio programming
still follows.

\textit{Pure Data}~\cite{puckette1996puredata} - Max's open-source sibling, built around the same
patching model and widely used in NIME instrument-building for its extensibility and free
licensing.

\textit{VCV Rack}~\cite{belt2017vcvrack} - a community-maintained open-source descendant of the same
patching idea applied to virtual modular synthesis, letting performers wire together emulated
Eurorack-style modules on screen.

\textit{EyesWeb}~\cite{camurri2000eyesweb} - Camurri et al.'s system for extracting expressive
movement qualities from full-body motion in real time, grounded in Laban's Effort theory and built
for interactive dance and music applications.

\textit{DAMPER}~\cite{bennett2007damper} - Bennett et al.'s force-feedback interface platform, built
on Laban's four Effort factors as the direct design basis for its haptic response.

Ward et al.~\cite{ward2008thereminists} - use Laban Effort - Flow and the Recuperation phase of
Effort phrasing in particular - as an analytic lens on theremin performers' movement, to inform how
future instruments might be designed around it.

Schacher~\cite{schacher2010motiontogesture} - argues for Laban-derived motion/effort elements as a
systematic basis for gesture-to-sound mapping in interactive dance, rather than an ad hoc or
purely descriptive analysis tool.

\subsection{Mapping Strategies}
Beyond specific instruments, a body of work addresses gesture-to-sound mapping as a design problem
in its own right, independent of any single controller's hardware.

Hunt and Wanderley~\cite{hunt2002mapping} - categorise gesture-to-synthesis mappings by complexity -
one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-one - and argue that richer, many-to-many mappings are what make an
interface feel like an instrument rather than a remote control.

\textit{Wekinator}~\cite{fiebrink2010wekinator} - Fiebrink and Cook's system for authoring a
gesture-to-sound relationship via interactive machine learning: a performer or designer supplies
example gestures and desired outputs, and regression learns the mapping, without either party
writing or wiring an explicit mapping function.

\section{Design and System Architecture}

\subsection{Initial Design Requirements}
Five requirements shaped the design from the outset, chosen to keep the instrument performable:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full-body, swingable staff form factor.} Not a handheld controller: the instrument
must support the reach, weight, and two-handed grip of an actual b\^o - roughly 1.5\,m long - not a
miniaturised stand-in for one.
    \item \textbf{Wireless sensing during performance.} A cable is acceptable only for charging
between sets; nothing should tether the performer to a fixed point on stage.
    \item \textbf{Live-editable mapping.} Switching or rewiring the mapping graph must not interrupt
audio output, so a change can be made mid-rehearsal without stopping the sound.
    \item \textbf{Mount-agnostic calibration.} Instrument behaviour must not depend on precisely
where the sensor is glued to the staff, since exact placement varies from build to build and cannot
be guaranteed reproducible by hand.
    \item \textbf{Low end-to-end latency.} The staff has to feel driven by the performer's motion,
not lagging behind it, which bounds how much time sensing, transport, and synthesis are together
allowed to take.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{The Build}
The sensor unit sits in a small OpenSCAD-parametrised enclosure, sized to the b\^o's diameter.
Because calibration is mount-agnostic, it can be placed anywhere along
the staff - in practice usually near one extremity, out of the performer's grip - and is secured
with zip ties rather than adhesive or drilling, so it can be repositioned or removed freely.

\subsection{Hardware}
Inside the enclosure, a SparkFun ESP32-S2 Thing Plus reads a 9-degrees-of-freedom (9-DOF) MPU-9250
IMU over the Inter-Integrated Circuit (I2C) bus at 100~Hz and packs orientation, acceleration, and
gyroscope readings into an OSC packet sent over the UDP on port 8000. The
firmware joins the host
computer's own Wi-Fi hotspot directly rather than a dedicated access point, which keeps setup to a
single pairing step at the cost of the range and interference sensitivity Discussion returns to
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection*{Orientation and Calibration}

\textbf{Rotation.} Orientation is tracked as a unit quaternion (no gimbal lock); rotating $v$ by $q$
gives $v' = q\,v\,q^{-1}$, with $q^{-1}$ the conjugate. Euler angles are derived only once, at the
end, for the handful of mappings that still need them.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::rotate}} \coderef{\texttt{conjugate}} \\
\coderef{\texttt{rotateVectorByQuaternion}}
\coderef{\texttt{toEuler}} \coderef{\texttt{multiply}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Calibration, stage 1 - which axis is ``forward''.} The sensor can be glued onto the staff at
any orientation, so each candidate axis $L \in \{\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\}$ is scored against three
reference poses $A,B,C$ (quaternions $q_A,q_B,q_C$, chosen so forward varies substantially between
them while up/right do not):
\[
v_P = \widehat{\mathrm{rotate}(L, q_P)}\,,\qquad e(L) = |v_A{\cdot}v_B| + |v_B{\cdot}v_C| +
|v_C{\cdot}v_A|
\]
A low $e(L)$ (near-orthogonal $v_A,v_B,v_C$) marks the genuine forward axis; restricted to
right-handed candidates,
\[
L^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{L\,:\,(v_A \times v_B)\cdot v_C < 0} e(L)
\]
gives the alignment quaternion $q_{\text{align}}$.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{computeAlignQuat}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Stage 1 failure modes.} \texttt{computeAlignQuat} reports failure when the three poses are
too close together or nearly coplanar to fix a physical axis with confidence, and the User Interface
(UI) surfaces this
as a "Calibration failed, try again" prompt.

\textbf{Calibration, stage 2 - fixing the twist.} The sensor could still be rotated around
$L^\ast$. The measured forward/up/right triad and the ideal instrument-frame triad are each
orthonormalized via Gram-Schmidt into $U$ and $W$; the aligning rotation is the orthogonal-Procrustes
closed form
\[
R = W U^{\top}\,,
\]
converted to a quaternion $q_{\text{corr}}$ via Shepperd's method.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{computeCorrection}}
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::fromMatrix}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Runtime.} Every live reading becomes instrument space via
$q' = q_{\text{corr}}\,(q_{\text{raw}}\,q_{\text{align}})$ - independent of where the sensor sits on
the staff.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{REMORAProcessor::getCalibratedQuat}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Gravity and dynamic acceleration.} The accelerometer always mixes gravity in with whatever
motion the performer is making. To separate the two, REMORA keeps a running estimate of which way
``down'' currently is, by smoothing the inverse-rotated world $+Z$ axis:
\[

\textbf{Laban features.} All four Laban Effort qualities reduce to the same shared one-pole
smoother,
\[
y_n = y_{n-1} + \alpha\,(x_n - y_{n-1})\,,\qquad \alpha \in (0,1]\,,
\]
applied to a derivative of either dynamic acceleration or gyroscope magnitude: Weight integrates
dynamic acceleration into a leaky velocity estimate (its magnitude, fast-attack/slow-release
enveloped) to capture forcefulness; Time is the clamped-positive rate of change of \emph{raw}
gyroscope magnitude, capturing suddenness; Space is the smoothed absolute dot product between
consecutive rotation-axis directions, capturing how consistently the staff spins around the same
axis versus wandering; Flow is the smoothed, clamped jerk of dynamic-acceleration magnitude, split
into \texttt{flow\_bound} (controlled) and $1-\texttt{flow\_bound}$ (free).

\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanTime}}

\end{flushright}

\textbf{Spin classification.} Is the staff spinning like a baton (vertical) or more like a fan blade
(horizontal)? Let $(\mathrm{axis}_x,\mathrm{axis}_y,\mathrm{axis}_z)$ be the $\alpha{=}0.35$
one-pole-smoothed rotation axis from above. The spin is classified vertical when
\[
\sqrt{\mathrm{axis}_x^2 + \mathrm{axis}_y^2}\ \gtrless\ |\mathrm{axis}_z|\,,
\]
i.e.\ when the horizontal-plane component outweighs the $z$ component. Spin direction
(clockwise/counter-clockwise, CW/CCW) then
comes either from the sign of the dominant in-plane axis component, or - in the Martial mappings -
from the sign of that axis's dot product against a reference heading latched once, the first time a
qualifying vertical spin happens after the mapping loads.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{updateSpinClassificationByAbsoluteComponent}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateSpinClassificationByReferenceAzimuth}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Facing.} Rather than compute a compass heading - which would need a magnetometer, and stages
are full of magnetic interference - REMORA asks a simpler question: is the staff pointing more
east-west or more north-south? Let $(\mathrm{dir}_x,\mathrm{dir}_y)$ be the horizontal-plane
components of whichever direction is currently relevant - the rotation axis during vertical spins,
the tip direction during horizontal ones. Facing is then just a hysteresis comparison,
\[
|\mathrm{dir}_x| \ \gtrless\ 1.15\cdot|\mathrm{dir}_y|\,,
\]
where the $1.15$ factor gives asymmetric switch points ($\approx\!48^\circ/42^\circ$) so the
classification doesn't flicker right at the boundary.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{updateFacingClassification}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Continuous spin count.} Full turns in the current spin, from $\lVert\omega\rVert\cdot\Delta t$
accumulated to $360^\circ$ per lap; resets whenever the spin classification changes.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{accumulateContinuousSpins}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Axial thrust detection.} Flags a stab/strike when the jerk of the tip-projected dynamic
acceleration crosses zero as its magnitude passes $1.5\text{g}$, gated against fast spins
($\lVert\omega\rVert < 90^\circ/\text{s}$) and cooled down between hits.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{detectAxialThrustPeaks}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Sensitivity of tuned constants.} Each constant above biases the instrument in a specific
direction. The gravity smoother's $\alpha{=}0.10$ trades under-reporting fast
motion (too high) against a lagging estimate that produces a spurious motion blip when the staff is
set down (too low). The spin-axis smoother's $\alpha{=}0.35$ plays the same role for Laban Space:
higher reads axis noise as ``inconsistent'' too readily, lower reads a genuinely wandering spin as
falsely focused. The facing hysteresis factor ($1.15$) sets the $\approx\!48^\circ/42^\circ$
dead-band; $1.0$ reintroduces boundary flicker, while a much larger factor makes facing changes
sluggish to register. The thrust detector's $1.5\text{g}$ threshold and $90^\circ/\text{s}$ gate trade
false triggers during forceful spins (too low) against missed soft stabs (too high).

\subsection{Software: Mapping Architecture}
\textit{\texttt{NodeGraph}/\texttt{NodeTypeRegistry}: four node categories - Source (IMU,\\
Laban features), Math (arithmetic, shaping, dynamics, lookup tables), Sink (synth parameters), and
Display (resizable, performer-facing live monitors, \S\ref{sec:live-patching}) - each a
small typed function registered once. A graph is nodes plus typed wiring, serialised to/from the
Extensible Markup Language (XML) as
a preset; \texttt{GraphMappingStrategy} makes the graph the only mapping path the synth talks to,
with no separate hand-written path.}

\subsubsection*{Graph Evaluation and Primitive Library}

\textbf{DAG evaluation.} A patch is a directed graph of typed nodes; \texttt{NodeGraph::connect}
provisionally adds an edge and recomputes a topological order with Kahn's algorithm via
\texttt{NodeGraph::recomputeTopoOrder}, reverting the edge if the graph fails to fully drain (a
closed cycle). Each audio block, \texttt{NodeGraph::evaluate} walks the cached order once, and every
node computes
\[
y_i = f_{\text{type}(n_i)}\big(x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}\,;\,\theta_i\big)
\]
where $n_i$ is the $i$-th node in topological order, $\text{type}(n_i)$ its registered node-type
function, $x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}$ its wired inputs, $\theta_i$ its parameters, and $y_i$ its output(s)
- so the whole patch is one composed function of the sensor frame, evaluated once per block with no
re-sorting on the audio thread.

\textbf{Primitive vocabulary.} Every node type is one small pure function (or, for stateful ones, a
function plus a scratch struct): arithmetic (add/subtract/multiply/divide/abs/max/min/equals),
shaping (mapRange, clamp, threshold, crossfade, quantizeSteps, sine, atan2, semitonesToHz), lookup
tables (a general 1-D table and the 3-boolean-selector table used by the \preset{Azimut} root table below),
and stateful dynamics (one-pole smoother, leaky integrator, retrigger envelope, hysteresis step,
derivative, latched smoother).
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{Graph::buildAllNodes}}
\end{flushright}

\subsubsection*{Azimut-Specific Transformations}
\textit{Shared by every Azimut-family preset.}

\textbf{Root-note table.} The spin-plane/direction/facing root-note table above is not an if/else
chain - it is literally an 8-entry lookup table (LUT) indexed by three booleans (\texttt{isVertical},
\texttt{isCCW}, \texttt{isFacingEast}) through the graph's generic 3-selector LUT node.

\textbf{Root morph.} The target semitone value is chased by a variable-rate one-pole filter whose
rate is
\[
\text{rate} = \max\Big(\mathrm{clamp}\big(\tfrac{\lVert\omega\rVert}{2000},\,0.005,\,0.2\big)\,,\ \
0.5\cdot\text{onsetEnv}\Big)
\]
where \texttt{onsetEnv} is a retrigger envelope (decay $0.90$) that fires the instant the staff
leaves rest - faster motion, or a fresh onset, morphs the root pitch in quicker.

\textbf{Filter sweep.} The spin count drives a phase modulator whose sine output is remapped onto
the filter's cutoff range and smoothed:
\begin{align*}
c_{\text{raw}} &=
\mathrm{mapRange}\big(\sin(1.5\cdot\text{spinCount})\,,\,[-1,1]\,,\,[400,20000]\text{Hz}\big) \\
\text{cutoff} &= \mathrm{onePole}\big(c_{\text{raw}}\,,\ \text{rate}{=}0.03\big)
\end{align*}
where $\text{spinCount}$ is the unsigned lap counter from Continuous Spin Count above,
$\mathrm{mapRange}$ linearly rescales its argument from the first interval to the second,
$c_{\text{raw}}$ the resulting unsmoothed cutoff target, and $\mathrm{onePole}$ the smoothing
primitive defined earlier.

\subsection{Graphical Interface: Live Patching as Performance Practice}
\label{sec:live-patching}
The node-graph editor is the instrument's primary authoring surface, not a debug tool: a pannable,
zoomable canvas on which each node renders as a coloured card - green for Source,
yellow for Sink, purple for Display, neutral for Math - labelled with its category and inline,
live-editable parameter fields; typed input/output pins along each card's edges accept
click-and-drag connections rendered as cubic-B\'ezier wires, colour-highlighted while dragging and
hit-testable afterward for rewiring or deletion via context menu, which for an existing wire also
offers jumping the view straight to its source or destination node, briefly highlighted on arrival
so the jump is easy to follow in a large patch. New nodes are added from a categorised picker at any
canvas position, each entry annotated with a short description of its behaviour; a search bar
over the same picker jumps directly to a node type by name. An auto-layout pass untangles a freshly
loaded preset into left-to-right source-to-sink columns. Hovering any node or pin surfaces its
current, live-updating value as a tooltip, and three dedicated Display node types - a numeric
readout, a level meter, and a scrolling scope - can be dropped inline on any wire and freely
resized, giving the performer a live, in-patch view of a signal without altering it (all three are
pure passthrough: the graph carries exactly the same value with or without one attached). A
dirty-state indicator tracks whether the active patch has diverged from its saved preset, with
one-click revert - so a performer can improvise a mapping change mid-rehearsal and either keep or
discard it, treating the mapping itself as something authored and iterated on at the same level as
the sound it produces. Each preset in the catalog also carries a short description,
surfaced in the preset selector, so a performer choosing between mappings mid-rehearsal is not
reading bare names.

\subsection{Software: Synthesis Engine}
Every mapping graph, regardless of preset, writes into the same \texttt{BoStaffSynth}: a shared
four-voice additive engine exposing one mapping-agnostic parameter surface (gain, drive, noise,
filter cutoff, pan, reverb send) that sink nodes address by name. Keeping the synthesis engine
itself ignorant of which preset is driving it is what lets a graph be swapped or rewired live
without reinitialising audio.

\subsubsection*{Additive Synthesis Engine} \
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{BoStaffSynth::processBlock}}
\coderef{\texttt{SynthVoice::tick}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Harmonic bank.} Each of the 4 voices sums 6 phase-accumulated sine partials at integer
multiples of a (vibrato- and chorus-modulated) fundamental, each with its own independently smoothed
target amplitude.

\textbf{Waveshaping.} A rational soft-clip saturator, applied per-voice when \texttt{driveAmt} $>0$:
\[
y = \frac{x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})}{1+\big|x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})\big|}
\]
where $x$ is the voice's summed harmonic-bank sample before shaping and \texttt{driveAmt} the
mapping-supplied drive amount.

\textbf{Resonator.} A short feedback comb (1800-sample delay, $0.35$ feedback, mixed $0.7$ dry /
$0.3$ wet) is layered onto every voice for a string-like coloration.

\textbf{Modulation.} Vibrato and tremolo are sinusoidal multipliers on pitch and gain respectively:
\[
f' = f\,\big(1+\text{depth}\cdot\sin\phi_v\big)
\qquad\qquad
g' = g\,\big(1-\text{depth}\cdot(0.5+0.5\sin\phi_t)\big)
\]
where $f, g$ are the fundamental frequency and master gain before modulation, $f', g'$ the modulated
values actually used, and $\phi_v, \phi_t$ the vibrato and tremolo Low-Frequency Oscillator (LFO)
phase accumulators (radians),
each advancing by its own rate every sample. A per-voice phase-offset sine (depth $0.003$, rate
$0.3$\,Hz) adds chorus detuning; chord changes cross-fade linearly between old and new semitone
offsets over $200$\,ms rather than jumping.

\textbf{Noise and filtering.} An xorshift32 pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) feeds a one-pole
lowpass (coefficient \texttt{noiseLpCoef}) before being mixed in at \texttt{noiseAmount}; the summed
stereo output passes through a JUCE state-variable Topology-Preserving Transform (TPT) lowpass, its
cutoff set directly each block from the graph's
\texttt{sink.lpfCutoffHz} (only range-clamped, not ramped), and an optional Schroeder-style
\texttt{juce::dsp::Reverb} send whose wet level \emph{is} ramped through a per-sample
\texttt{SmoothedValue} to avoid zippering when the active mapping changes.

\textbf{Pitch/frequency conversion.}
\[
f = f_{\text{root}} \cdot 2^{\,\text{semitones}/12}
\]
where $f_{\text{root}}$ is the mapping's current root frequency in Hz and $\text{semitones}$ the
signed offset applied to it, giving the equal-tempered result $f$.

\subsection{Development Iterations}
The node-graph engine above did not arrive fully formed; it is the result of three iterations in
which generalisation followed rather than preceded hand-built mapping design. The first was
firmware reading the IMU and streaming OSC into a minimal receiver, driving a single hardcoded
tilt-to-pitch mapping, purely to prove the sensor-to-sound pipeline end to end. The second built
the eleven named mappings one at a time as their own C++ classes, each adding a new gesture
vocabulary (chords, Laban Effort, world-frame facing, transient detection) on top of the shared
\texttt{StaffMotionAnalyzer} - at which point a pattern became visible: every one of them was
really just arithmetic, shaping, and a handful of stateful primitives wired together in a fixed
pattern. The third iteration rebuilt the mapping layer as the small generic graph engine described
above, porting all eleven prior mappings over as presets rather than deleting them.

\subsection{Evidence from the Implemented System}
Claims (\ref{claim:dag}), (\ref{claim:presets}), and (\ref{claim:calibration}) are, at present, supported by implementation-level
evidence - the system as built and run by the author during development - rather than by an
independent observer. The graph evaluator provisionally adds and removes edges, recomputes a
topological order, and drives synthesis every audio block without recompilation or an audio dropout,
exercised every time a preset is loaded or a wire is changed. Table~\ref{tab:preset-nodes}'s per-preset node counts are
read directly off the constructed graphs, so claim (\ref{claim:presets})'s "eleven mappings, one catalog" result is a
property of the code, not a reported impression - though the catalog grew alongside those eleven
mappings, so its sufficiency for a mapping designed independently of the project remains open
(\S\ref{sec:named-mappings}). Claim (\ref{claim:calibration})'s mount-independence follows from the two-stage calibration
derivation holding for any physically valid sensor placement, a property we can state
mathematically but have not yet stress-tested by remounting the enclosure at several positions.
Claim (\ref{claim:facing}) is the weakest of the four: the facing heuristic runs correctly in every development
session, but "correctly" here means matching the author's own sense of where the staff was
pointing, not a logged, ground-truth comparison - drift is bounded to short sessions by assumption,
not measurement. No human-subject data has been collected for this instrument, and none of the four
claims should be read as validated by user testing until the protocol below, or something like it,
has actually been run.

\subsection{Planned Performer Evaluation}
To test the parts of these claims that are inherently about performer experience rather than code
behaviour - whether the graph is rewireable by someone other than its author, whether a
Laban-driven preset reads as more physically legible than a simple one, whether the node picker
scales to larger patches - we plan a semi-structured session with performers who have some staff,
baton, or movement-flow background but no prior exposure to REMORA. The intended structure: a short
calibration walkthrough; free play across a small span of existing presets chosen to range from a
minimal mapping to a fully Laban-driven one; a guided patching task (retuning an exposed parameter,
then rewiring which motion feature drives a sink); and a closing semi-structured interview.
Candidate measures include calibration time, time to complete the guided rewire unassisted, think-
aloud commentary during free play, a preference judgement between at least two presets with a
reason, and any node-picker search difficulties observed or reported. Each measure is chosen to
speak to a specific claim in Table~\ref{tab:claims-evidence} rather than to general usability. This protocol - not results -
is what we currently have; running it, with informed consent handled as described in the Ethical
Statement, is the immediate next step for this work.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Advantages and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The generalisation into a graph buys expressiveness and shareability - eleven previously hand-coded
mappings collapse into presets of one small dataflow language - at the cost of a patching step that
performers used to fixed instruments do not otherwise have to take. The limitations below are ones
the current build genuinely carries, not future work described in present tense.

\textbf{Network deployment is manual.} REMORA joins the host device's own Wi-Fi hotspot rather than
a dedicated access point, trading range and interference robustness for a one-step pairing setup.
That convenience hides a real cost: the hotspot's Service Set Identifier (SSID)/password and the OSC
destination IP address are constants compiled into the firmware. A phone hotspot
does not guarantee the same IP across sessions, and pairing with a different host network means a
different SSID and password entirely -
either case currently requires editing the firmware source and reflashing the microcontroller over
the USB before the staff will talk to the plugin again. There is no runtime
Wi-Fi configuration, no
network discovery of the receiving host, and no feedback if the join fails silently.

\textbf{Single IMU.} All orientation and motion-quality features come from one IMU with no
magnetometer fusion; heading is deliberately kept relative to the calibration pose rather than
magnetic north, since the magnetometer is unreliable this close to the staff's own electronics and
typical stage rigs. That keeps facing free of magnetic interference, but it also means REMORA has no
absolute reference at all - any yaw drift accumulated since calibration is invisible and
uncorrected, and the facing feature is only ever as good as that session's calibration
(\S\ref{sec:evaluation} bounds claim~\ref{claim:facing} to session lengths of tens of minutes on this basis, not a
measured guarantee). Nothing in the current implementation prevents drift from growing unboundedly
over a longer, uninterrupted rehearsal or set, and no re-calibration-on-demand gesture currently
exists to correct it mid-performance; ground-truth heading logging would need to be added to the
planned performer evaluation before that bound could be tightened.

\textbf{Graph interface complexity.} The node-graph editor is expressive for whoever is building or
modifying a mapping, but it exposes that same complexity to a performer who only wants to select and
play an existing preset - there is no simplified performance-only view that hides the graph until
asked for. The interface currently serves instrument-builders and patch-authors more directly than
first-time performers, and whether that cost is acceptable is a question the still-missing user
study would need to answer.

\textbf{Remaining software robustness gaps.} A few implementation-level issues would need attention
before REMORA is exposed to inputs beyond the author's own testing: incoming OSC messages are read
without confirming their argument types match what the receiver expects, dropped or reordered
packets are not detected as such, and the handoff of a sensor snapshot from the network thread to
the audio thread retries in a tight spin loop rather than degrading gracefully under contention.

\textbf{Hardware.} The enclosure carries a 2000\,mAh LiPo battery, sized to comfortably outlast a
single rehearsal or set at the firmware's steady 100\,Hz sampling rate, but runtime under REMORA's
actual firmware has not been measured: no discharge test has been logged to date. That measurement
- ideally several runs to a defined cutoff voltage, reported with variance - is needed before any
runtime claim can be made or the battery sized with confidence for a full day of back-to-back
performances.

\subsection{Future Improvements}

\textbf{Wireless transport.} The hotspot-join scheme in \S\ref{sec:limitations} could be replaced
with ESP-NOW: the staff-mounted ESP32 would send its sensor packets directly, peer-to-peer, to a
second ESP32 tethered over USB to the host computer, which forwards them to the plugin over serial
or a loopback OSC bridge. That removes the SSID/password/IP baked into firmware entirely - ESP-NOW
pairs by MAC address rather than joining a network - and sidesteps the "new
hotspot means reflash"
problem, at the cost of adding a second microcontroller to the setup.

\textbf{Other extensions.} Gesture-triggered mode switching, a merged rest/motion mapping, a two-IMU
dual-tip configuration, further Laban Effort mapping targets, machine learning based discrete
gesture recognition,
and eventually exposing the node graph itself as a shareable/community patch format.

\section{Conclusions}
REMORA is a wireless, IMU-driven b\^o staff instrument, but its contribution is architectural rather
than being one more fixed gesture-to-sound mapping: the same small dataflow language - source,
math, and sink node primitives, evaluated as a DAG once per audio block and rewireable without
recompiling - reproduces all eleven mapping strategies developed over the course of the project as
ordinary, live-editable presets (Table~\ref{tab:preset-nodes}), from a minimal tilt-to-pitch patch to the fully
Laban-driven \preset{Martial} and \preset{Speed Gate} mappings, grounded in an explicit, code-traceable account of
calibration, orientation tracking, and movement-quality extraction (\S\ref{sec:named-mappings}).

What remains uncertain is exactly what \S\ref{sec:evaluation} makes explicit: whether these claims hold for a
performer other than the author, and whether the primitive catalog and facing estimate fare under
conditions implementation-level evidence alone cannot settle. The most important next step for this
work is therefore not another architectural extension but running the performer evaluation protocol
outlined in \S\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Ethical Statement}
\textbf{Accessibility.} REMORA assumes a range of mobility and two-handed grip strength consistent
with swinging a b\^o staff; it is not currently adapted for performers who cannot support or swing a
1--1.5\,kg full-length staff, and no alternative mounting or reduced-effort variant has been
explored - a real limitation of the instrument's physical form, not merely of the present build.
The skill barrier is not uniform across the preset catalog, though: \preset{Simple} asks only for basic
tilt and roll, while the Laban-driven presets (\preset{Martial}, \preset{Azimut}) reward accumulated
staff-handling skill. Because every preset is authored in the same shared patching language
(\S\ref{sec:named-mappings}), a facilitator can also build a reduced-effort mapping for someone
without a staff background - a partial mitigation on the skill axis, not a solution to the physical
one above.

\textbf{Environmental impact.} The staff-mounted hardware - a 3D-printed/OpenSCAD-parametrised
enclosure, a rechargeable LiPo battery, an ESP32-S2 microcontroller, and an MPU-9250 IMU - is held
together with zip ties rather than adhesive (\S\ref{sec:limitations}, The Build), so the enclosure
and electronics can be removed, reused, or recycled without damaging the staff. No lifecycle or
embodied-carbon analysis of the components has been performed.

\textbf{Socio-economic fairness.} REMORA is built entirely on free/libre and open-source tools -
JUCE, PlatformIO, and OpenSCAD - with an inexpensive, widely available microcontroller and IMU,
keeping the reproduction cost and toolchain barrier low relative to proprietary DMI environments; the source and design files referenced in Supplementary Material
are what make that reproduction actually possible.

\textbf{Human participants.} No human-participant data has been collected to date; all reported
observations are the first author's own, and the performer evaluation outlined in
\S\ref{sec:evaluation} has not yet been conducted. When it is run, participants will give informed
consent, be referred to only by anonymised identifiers, and be free to withdraw at any point.

\textbf{Use of Artificial Intelligence.} We disclose that a locally-run large language model (Mistral 7B) was used to help draft and edit
portions of this manuscript's prose under the first author's direction and review. This disclosure
applies only to the writing of this paper: REMORA's firmware, plugin, and mapping code were designed
and written entirely by the first author without AI code generation.
