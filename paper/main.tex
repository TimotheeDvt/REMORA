\begin{abstract}
REMORA turns a martial arts staff (b\^o) into a musical instrument: spins, tilts, and strikes become
sound in real time. A wireless sensor unit on the staff tracks its motion and sends it to a
companion audio plugin, which we built around a simple idea - instead of hard-wiring one fixed
gesture-to-sound behaviour, the mapping itself is a small patch of connected building blocks that
the performer can see and rewire live, on a visual canvas inside the plugin, much like a modular
synthesiser. We show that this same small set of building blocks is enough to reproduce, as editable
patches, everything from simple tilt-controlled pitch to compound movement-quality mappings inspired
by Laban Movement Analysis. This paper describes the instrument's hardware, its self-calibration
method, the mathematics behind turning raw orientation into musical control, and the live patching
interface itself, situates it against prior staff-shaped, baton, and visual-patching instruments,
and - grounded in implementation-level evidence together with a protocol for the performer
evaluation still to be run - reflects on what it means to make an instrument's gestural vocabulary
an explicit, editable part of the instrument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection*{Motivation and Background}
REMORA grew out of the first author's parallel practice in martial arts and music, and a frustration
shared by many hand-built digital musical instruments: once a gesture vocabulary is designed and
coded, changing it means rebuilding the instrument rather than performing with it. That friction is
the paper's real starting point - not which single mapping best suits a staff, but whether a staff
instrument's gestural vocabulary could instead be something the performer authors and revises live,
the way a modular-synthesis patch is built and rebuilt on a rack.

The b\^o staff itself sits in a gap NIME has left largely unexplored: a full-body, swung, two-handed
controller with its own rotational inertia and reach, distinct from the small handheld or
body-mounted objects most inertial-sensor DMIs use. Pairing that underused controller form with a
mapping layer that can be reshaped at performance time, rather than fixed at design time, is the
combination this paper investigates.

\subsection*{Objectives of the Study}
This article supports four claims, each argued based on the system as it currently operates rather than being proposed as future work:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{claim:dag} A small dataflow language - source, math, and sink node primitives - is embedded directly in
the audio plugin and evaluated once per audio block as a directed acyclic graph (DAG); the performer
rewires it live, without recompiling any code.
\item \label{claim:presets} That same handful of primitives is expressive enough to reproduce eleven previously hand-coded
mapping strategies as ordinary presets: the mapping design space explored by hand over the course of
the project turns out to be spanned by a fairly compact algebra of building blocks.
\item \label{claim:facing} A magnetometer-free, world-frame heading estimate (``facing'') is derived purely from the
calibrated orientation stream, eluding the magnetic interference a stage rig would otherwise cause -
a claim we bound to session lengths on the order of tens of minutes, since uncorrected gyroscopic
yaw drift is expected to accumulate over longer, uninterrupted play (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).
\item \label{claim:calibration} A mount-agnostic calibration transform lets the same instrument logic run correctly regardless
of where the sensor is physically mounted on the staff.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Scope and Applications}
\textit{REMORA targets solo live performance with a single staff and a single IMU - not an
installation piece, not a multi-performer controller (at least not yet, see Future Improvements).
Worth being upfront about what's out of scope for this paper too: no formal user study, no
controlled comparison against other staff instruments, and no claim that patching a mapping is
easier to learn than a fixed one - only that the capability exists and is expressive.}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection*{Staff, Rod, and Baton DMIs}
The T-Stick~\cite{malloch2007tstick} is the closest prior instrument to REMORA's physical form: a
family of rod-shaped controllers sensing finger position and pressure along the shaft via capacitive
touch, together with accelerometer-derived shake and tap gestures. Crucially, the T-Stick project
pairs that sensing with an external, reconfigurable mapping layer (the digital-orchestra/libmapper
tools built alongside it), so the gesture vocabulary is not permanently fixed to the hardware either
- but that rewiring happens in supporting software outside the instrument, authored by a mapping
designer between pieces, rather than as a performer-facing surface embedded in the instrument
itself during play. REMORA differs from the T-Stick on two axes at once: its full-body two-handed
swung form (closer to a bô or fighting staff than a handheld rod), and a live-patchable mapping
graph built directly into the audio plugin the performer plays through, rather than a separate
mapping tool.

Conducting batons form a second, older lineage of rod-shaped expressive controllers. Marrin and
Paradiso's Digital Baton~\cite{marrin1997digitalbaton} combined accelerometers, infrared position
tracking, and a pressure-sensitive tip to capture continuous conducting gesture, and the companion
Conductor's Jacket~\cite{marrin1998conductorsjacket} extended the same goal - recording expressive,
continuous musical gesture rather than discrete note events - to a wearable, full-body EMG and
motion rig. Both systems used a fixed mapping, hand-engineered by the researchers for conducting
gesture specifically; REMORA shares the goal of capturing continuous, qualitative gesture (via
Laban Effort features, \S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive} and Table~1) but treats the mapping from that
gesture to sound as something the performer edits rather than something fixed by the instrument
builder in advance.

Swung or spun full-body objects are a comparatively thin category at NIME: the clearest match is
Nam's Musical Poi (mPoi)~\cite{nam2013mpoi}, a sensor-augmented poi that generates sound from
swing motion. mPoi confirms that spun/swung objects are a recognised, if under-explored, DMI form,
but it is a compact, single-mapping instrument without a reconfigurable mapping layer or a
movement-quality vocabulary comparable to Laban Effort. REMORA occupies the same underexplored
niche of full-body, swung, two-handed controllers but combines it with the T-Stick's ambition of
a reconfigurable gesture vocabulary and the conducting-baton lineage's focus on continuous
expressive quality rather than discrete triggers - a combination we are not aware of in prior
staff-, rod-, or swung-object DMIs.

\subsection*{Inertial Sensing and Visual Patching in NIME}
Visual dataflow patching is a much older paradigm than any of the staff instruments above. Max
originated as Puckette's Patcher~\cite{puckette1988patcher} and has been maintained and extended
commercially for over three decades, a history Puckette himself traces in
``Max at Seventeen''~\cite{puckette2002maxatseventeen}; Pure Data~\cite{puckette1996puredata} is
its open-source sibling, built around the same boxes-and-patch-cords model of unit generators wired
together and rewired live. VCV Rack~\cite{belt2017vcvrack} is a more recent, community-maintained
open-source descendant of the same idea applied to virtual modular synthesis. REMORA's node-graph
editor (\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}) draws directly on this patching lineage - typed nodes, typed
wires, live rewiring without stopping audio - but repurposes it specifically as a
\emph{gesture-to-sound mapping layer} rather than a general-purpose signal-processing environment:
REMORA's nodes represent motion features, shaping functions, and synthesis-parameter sinks, not
arbitrary DSP, and the graph lives inside a single instrument's plugin rather than functioning as a
standalone environment a performer would build an entire instrument in.

Using Laban Movement Analysis as a source of mapping features has real precedent in NIME and
related venues. Camurri et al.'s EyesWeb~\cite{camurri2000eyesweb} established a technical template
for extracting expressive movement qualities from full-body motion in real time, explicitly
grounded in Laban's Effort theory, for interactive dance and music systems. Bennett et
al.'s DAMPER~\cite{bennett2007damper} used Laban's four Effort factors directly as the design basis
for a force-feedback interface platform, Ward et al.~\cite{ward2008thereminists} used Laban Effort
- Flow and the Recuperation phase of Effort phrasing in particular - as an analytic lens on theremin
performers' movement to inform instrument design, and Schacher~\cite{schacher2010motiontogesture}
argues explicitly for Laban-derived motion/effort elements as a systematic basis for gesture-to-sound
mapping in interactive dance. REMORA continues this line by computing the same four Effort
qualities (Weight, Time, Space, Flow; Table~1) as real-time IMU-derived features, but where the
cited work treats Effort primarily as an analysis or fixed-mapping-design tool, REMORA exposes those
qualities as first-class typed nodes a performer can wire into an arbitrary sink at performance
time - combining EyesWeb's Effort-feature-extraction approach with the Max/Pd/VCV-style repatchable
dataflow paradigm inside a single embedded, performer-facing instrument, which is the specific
combination we have not found addressed together in prior work.

\section{Design and System Architecture}

\subsection*{Initial Design Requirements}
Five requirements shaped the design from the outset, chosen to keep the instrument performable
rather than merely functional on a workbench:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full-body, swingable staff form factor.} Not a handheld controller: the instrument
must support the reach, weight, and two-handed grip of an actual b\^o, not a miniaturised stand-in
for one.
    \item \textbf{Wireless sensing during performance.} A cable is acceptable only for charging
between sets; nothing should tether the performer to a fixed point on stage.
    \item \textbf{Live-editable mapping.} Switching or rewiring the mapping graph must not interrupt
audio output, so a change can be made mid-rehearsal without stopping the sound.
    \item \textbf{Mount-agnostic calibration.} Instrument behaviour must not depend on precisely
where the sensor is glued to the staff, since exact placement varies from build to build and cannot
be guaranteed reproducible by hand.
    \item \textbf{Low end-to-end latency.} The staff has to feel driven by the performer's motion,
not lagging behind it, which bounds how much time sensing, transport, and synthesis are together
allowed to take.
\end{itemize}

REMORA's architecture has two parts, matching the physical split between staff and host: firmware
handles sensing and transport, and the plugin handles everything downstream of the incoming OSC
stream, from calibration through to the audio it produces. Figure~1 lays out that split directly,

\section{Implementation}

\subsection*{The Build}
The sensor unit sits in a small OpenSCAD-parametrised enclosure, sized to the b\^o's diameter rather
than assuming a fixed one. Because calibration is mount-agnostic, the enclosure can be placed
anywhere along the staff; in practice it is usually mounted near one extremity, out of the way of
the performer's grip. It is secured with three zip ties passing through channels in the
casing - no adhesive or drilling - so it can be repositioned or removed freely. The same zip ties
hold the two enclosure halves closed, together with screws that additionally clamp the internal
electronics in place so nothing shifts under swing.

\subsection*{Hardware}
Inside the enclosure, a SparkFun ESP32-S2 Thing Plus reads a 9-DOF MPU-9250 IMU over I2C
(Inter-Integrated Circuit) at 100~Hz and packs orientation, acceleration, and gyroscope readings into
an OSC packet sent over UDP (User Datagram Protocol) on port 8000. The firmware joins the host
computer's own Wi-Fi hotspot directly rather than a dedicated access point, which keeps setup to a
single pairing step at the cost of the range and interference sensitivity Discussion returns to
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsubsection*{Orientation and Calibration}

\textbf{Rotation.} Orientation is tracked as a unit quaternion (no gimbal lock); rotating $v$ by $q$
gives $v' = q\,v\,q^{-1}$, with $q^{-1}$ the conjugate. Euler angles are derived only once, at the
end, for the handful of mappings that still need them.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::rotate}} \coderef{\texttt{conjugate}} \\
\coderef{\texttt{rotateVectorByQuaternion}}
\coderef{\texttt{toEuler}} \coderef{\texttt{multiply}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Calibration, stage 1 - which axis is ``forward''.} The sensor can be glued onto the staff at
any orientation, so each candidate axis $L \in \{\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\}$ is scored against three
reference poses $A,B,C$ (quaternions $q_A,q_B,q_C$, chosen so forward varies substantially between
them while up/right do not):
\[
v_P = \widehat{\mathrm{rotate}(L, q_P)}\,,\qquad e(L) = |v_A{\cdot}v_B| + |v_B{\cdot}v_C| +
|v_C{\cdot}v_A|
\]
A low $e(L)$ (near-orthogonal $v_A,v_B,v_C$) marks the genuine forward axis; restricted to
right-handed candidates,
\[
L^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{L\,:\,(v_A \times v_B)\cdot v_C < 0} e(L)
\]
gives the alignment quaternion $q_{\text{align}}$.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{computeAlignQuat}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Stage 1 failure modes.} \texttt{computeAlignQuat} reports failure when the three poses are
too close together or nearly coplanar to fix a physical axis with confidence, and the UI surfaces this
as a "Calibration failed, try again" prompt.

\textbf{Calibration, stage 2 - fixing the twist.} The sensor could still be rotated around
$L^\ast$. The measured forward/up/right triad and the ideal instrument-frame triad are each
orthonormalized via Gram-Schmidt into $U$ and $W$; the aligning rotation is the orthogonal-Procrustes
closed form
\[
R = W U^{\top}\,,
\]
converted to a quaternion $q_{\text{corr}}$ via Shepperd's method.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{computeCorrection}}
\coderef{\texttt{MathHelpers::fromMatrix}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Runtime.} Every live reading becomes instrument space via
$q' = q_{\text{corr}}\,(q_{\text{raw}}\,q_{\text{align}})$ - independent of where the sensor sits on
the staff.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{REMORAProcessor::getCalibratedQuat}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Timestep.} $\Delta t$ is the time between two packets, clamped to a $0.1$-$200\,\text{ms}$

\textbf{Tip vector and rotation axis.} The tip direction\\ $p_n = \mathrm{rotate}((1,0,0), q_n)$ is
buffered over the last three samples to derive tip velocity and, from that, the axis the staff is
currently spinning around.
\begin{flushright}

\textbf{Gravity and dynamic acceleration.} The accelerometer always mixes gravity in with whatever
motion the performer is making. To separate the two, REMORA keeps a running estimate of which way
``down'' currently is, by smoothing the inverse-rotated world $+Z$ axis:
\[
g_n = \mathrm{onePole}\big(g_{n-1},\ \mathrm{rotate}(+Z,\, q_n^{-1}),\ \alpha{=}0.10\big)
\]
Subtracting this gravity estimate from the raw reading leaves just the motion: $a_{\text{dyn}} =
a_{\text{raw}} - g$.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{updateGravityVector}}
\coderef{\texttt{calculateDynamicAcceleration}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Laban features.} All four Laban Effort qualities reduce to the same shared one-pole
smoother,
\[
y_n = y_{n-1} + \alpha\,(x_n - y_{n-1})\,,\qquad \alpha \in (0,1]\,,
\]
applied to a derivative of either dynamic acceleration or gyroscope magnitude: Weight integrates
dynamic acceleration into a leaky velocity estimate (its magnitude, fast-attack/slow-release
enveloped) to capture forcefulness; Time is the clamped-positive rate of change of \emph{raw}
gyroscope magnitude, capturing suddenness; Space is the smoothed absolute dot product between
consecutive rotation-axis directions, capturing how consistently the staff spins around the same
axis versus wandering; Flow is the smoothed, clamped jerk of dynamic-acceleration magnitude, split
into \texttt{flow\_bound} (controlled) and $1-\texttt{flow\_bound}$ (free).

\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanWeight}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanTime}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanSpace}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateLabanFlow}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Spin classification.} Is the staff spinning like a baton (vertical) or more like a fan blade
(horizontal)? Let $(\mathrm{axis}_x,\mathrm{axis}_y,\mathrm{axis}_z)$ be the $\alpha{=}0.35$
one-pole-smoothed rotation axis from above. The spin is classified vertical when
\[
\sqrt{\mathrm{axis}_x^2 + \mathrm{axis}_y^2}\ \gtrless\ |\mathrm{axis}_z|\,,
\]
i.e.\ when the horizontal-plane component outweighs the $z$ component. Spin direction (CW/CCW) then
comes either from the sign of the dominant in-plane axis component, or - in the Bozendo mappings -
from the sign of that axis's dot product against a reference heading latched once, the first time a
qualifying vertical spin happens after the mapping loads.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{updateSpinClassificationByAbsoluteComponent}}
\coderef{\texttt{updateSpinClassificationByReferenceAzimuth}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Facing.} Rather than compute a compass heading - which would need a magnetometer, and stages
are full of magnetic interference - REMORA asks a simpler question: is the staff pointing more
east-west or more north-south? Let $(\mathrm{dir}_x,\mathrm{dir}_y)$ be the horizontal-plane
components of whichever direction is currently relevant - the rotation axis during vertical spins,
the tip direction during horizontal ones. Facing is then just a hysteresis comparison,
\[
|\mathrm{dir}_x| \ \gtrless\ 1.15\cdot|\mathrm{dir}_y|\,,
\]
where the $1.15$ factor gives asymmetric switch points ($\approx\!48^\circ/42^\circ$) so the
classification doesn't flicker right at the boundary.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{updateFacingClassification}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Continuous spin count.} Full turns in the current spin, from $\lVert\omega\rVert\cdot\Delta t$
accumulated to $360^\circ$ per lap; resets whenever the spin classification changes.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{accumulateContinuousSpins}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Axial thrust detection.} Flags a stab/strike when the jerk of the tip-projected dynamic
acceleration crosses zero as its magnitude passes $1.5\text{g}$, gated against fast spins
($\lVert\omega\rVert < 90^\circ/\text{s}$) and cooled down between hits.
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{detectAxialThrustPeaks}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Sensitivity of tuned constants.} None of the constants above were fit to data; each biases the
instrument in a specific direction. The gravity smoother's $\alpha{=}0.10$ trades under-reporting fast
motion (too high) against a lagging estimate that produces a spurious motion blip when the staff is
set down (too low). The spin-axis smoother's $\alpha{=}0.35$ plays the same role for Laban Space:
higher reads axis noise as ``inconsistent'' too readily, lower reads a genuinely wandering spin as
falsely focused. The facing hysteresis factor ($1.15$) sets the $\approx\!48^\circ/42^\circ$
dead-band; $1.0$ reintroduces boundary flicker, while a much larger factor makes facing changes
sluggish to register. The thrust detector's $1.5\text{g}$ threshold and $90^\circ/\text{s}$ gate trade
false triggers during forceful spins (too low) against missed soft stabs (too high). None of this has
been swept against recorded data; Discussion returns to it alongside the missing perceptual
evaluation.

\subsection*{Software: Mapping Architecture}
\textit{\texttt{NodeGraph}/\texttt{NodeTypeRegistry}: three node categories - Source (IMU,\\
Laban features), Math (arithmetic, shaping, dynamics, lookup tables), Sink (synth parameters) - each a
small typed function registered once. A graph is nodes plus typed wiring, serialised to/from XML as
a preset; \texttt{GraphMappingStrategy} makes the graph the only mapping path the synth talks to,
with no separate hand-written path.}

\subsubsection*{Graph Evaluation and Primitive Library}

\textbf{DAG evaluation.} A patch is a directed graph of typed nodes; \texttt{NodeGraph::connect}
provisionally adds an edge and recomputes a topological order with Kahn's algorithm via
\texttt{NodeGraph::recomputeTopoOrder}, reverting the edge if the graph fails to fully drain (a
closed cycle). Each audio block, \texttt{NodeGraph::evaluate} walks the cached order once, and every
node computes
\[
y_i = f_{\text{type}(n_i)}\big(x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}\,;\,\theta_i\big)
\]
where $n_i$ is the $i$-th node in topological order, $\text{type}(n_i)$ its registered node-type
function, $x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,m}$ its wired inputs, $\theta_i$ its parameters, and $y_i$ its output(s)
- so the whole patch is one composed function of the sensor frame, evaluated once per block with no
re-sorting on the audio thread.

\textbf{Primitive vocabulary.} Every node type is one small pure function (or, for stateful ones, a
function plus a scratch struct): arithmetic (add/subtract/multiply/divide/abs/max/min/equals),
shaping (mapRange, clamp, threshold, crossfade, quantizeSteps, sine, atan2, semitonesToHz), lookup
tables (a general 1-D table and the 3-boolean-selector table used by the Azimut root table below),
and stateful dynamics (one-pole smoother, leaky integrator, retrigger envelope, hysteresis step,
derivative, latched smoother).
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{Graph::buildAllNodes}}
\end{flushright}

\subsubsection*{Azimut-Specific Transformations}
\textit{Shared by every Azimut-family preset.}

\textbf{Root-note table.} The spin-plane/direction/facing root-note table above is not an if/else
chain - it is literally an 8-entry lookup table indexed by three booleans (\texttt{isVertical},
\texttt{isCCW}, \texttt{isFacingEast}) through the graph's generic 3-selector LUT node.

\textbf{Root morph.} The target semitone value is chased by a variable-rate one-pole filter whose
rate is
\[
\text{rate} = \max\Big(\mathrm{clamp}\big(\tfrac{\lVert\omega\rVert}{2000},\,0.005,\,0.2\big)\,,\ \
0.5\cdot\text{onsetEnv}\Big)
\]
where \texttt{onsetEnv} is a retrigger envelope (decay $0.90$) that fires the instant the staff
leaves rest - faster motion, or a fresh onset, morphs the root pitch in quicker.

\textbf{Filter sweep.} The spin count drives a phase modulator whose sine output is remapped onto
the filter's cutoff range and smoothed:
\begin{align*}
c_{\text{raw}} &=
\mathrm{mapRange}\big(\sin(1.5\cdot\text{spinCount})\,,\,[-1,1]\,,\,[400,20000]\text{Hz}\big) \\
\text{cutoff} &= \mathrm{onePole}\big(c_{\text{raw}}\,,\ \text{rate}{=}0.03\big)
\end{align*}
where $\text{spinCount}$ is the unsigned lap counter from Continuous Spin Count above,
$\mathrm{mapRange}$ linearly rescales its argument from the first interval to the second,
$c_{\text{raw}}$ the resulting unsmoothed cutoff target, and $\mathrm{onePole}$ the smoothing
primitive defined earlier.

\subsection*{Graphical Interface: Live Patching as Performance Practice}
The node-graph editor is the instrument's primary authoring surface, not a debug tool: a pannable,
zoomable canvas on which each node renders as a coloured card - green for Source,
yellow for Sink, neutral for Math - labelled with its category and inline, live-editable parameter
fields; typed input/output pins along each card's edges accept click-and-drag connections rendered
as cubic-B\'ezier wires, colour-highlighted while dragging and hit-testable afterward for rewiring
or deletion via context menu. New nodes are added from a categorised picker at any canvas position;
an auto-layout pass untangles a freshly loaded preset into left-to-right source-to-sink columns. A
dirty-state indicator tracks whether the active patch has diverged from its saved preset, with
one-click revert - so a performer can improvise a mapping change mid-rehearsal and either keep or
discard it, treating the mapping itself as something authored and iterated on at the same level as
the sound it produces.

\subsection*{Software: Synthesis Engine}
Every mapping graph, regardless of preset, writes into the same \texttt{BoStaffSynth}: a shared
four-voice additive engine exposing one mapping-agnostic parameter surface (gain, drive, noise,
filter cutoff, pan, reverb send) that sink nodes address by name. Keeping the synthesis engine
itself ignorant of which preset is driving it is what lets a graph be swapped or rewired live
without reinitialising audio.

\subsubsection*{Additive Synthesis Engine} \
\begin{flushright}
\coderef{\texttt{BoStaffSynth::processBlock}}
\coderef{\texttt{SynthVoice::tick}}
\end{flushright}

\textbf{Harmonic bank.} Each of the 4 voices sums 6 phase-accumulated sine partials at integer
multiples of a (vibrato- and chorus-modulated) fundamental, each with its own independently smoothed
target amplitude.

\textbf{Waveshaping.} A rational soft-clip saturator, applied per-voice when \texttt{driveAmt} $>0$:
\[
y = \frac{x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})}{1+\big|x\,(1+\text{driveAmt})\big|}
\]
where $x$ is the voice's summed harmonic-bank sample before shaping and \texttt{driveAmt} the
mapping-supplied drive amount.

\textbf{Resonator.} A short feedback comb (1800-sample delay, $0.35$ feedback, mixed $0.7$ dry /
$0.3$ wet) is layered onto every voice for a string-like coloration.

\textbf{Modulation.} Vibrato and tremolo are sinusoidal multipliers on pitch and gain respectively:
\[
f' = f\,\big(1+\text{depth}\cdot\sin\phi_v\big)
\qquad\qquad
g' = g\,\big(1-\text{depth}\cdot(0.5+0.5\sin\phi_t)\big)
\]
where $f, g$ are the fundamental frequency and master gain before modulation, $f', g'$ the modulated
values actually used, and $\phi_v, \phi_t$ the vibrato and tremolo LFO phase accumulators (radians),
each advancing by its own rate every sample. A per-voice phase-offset sine (depth $0.003$, rate
$0.3$\,Hz) adds chorus detuning; chord changes cross-fade linearly between old and new semitone
offsets over $200$\,ms rather than jumping.

\textbf{Noise and filtering.} An xorshift32 PRNG feeds a one-pole lowpass (coefficient
\texttt{noiseLpCoef}) before being mixed in at \texttt{noiseAmount}; the summed stereo output passes
through a JUCE state-variable TPT lowpass, its cutoff set directly each block from the graph's
\texttt{sink.lpfCutoffHz} (only range-clamped, not ramped), and an optional Schroeder-style
\texttt{juce::dsp::Reverb} send whose wet level \emph{is} ramped through a per-sample
\texttt{SmoothedValue} to avoid zippering when the active mapping changes.

\textbf{Pitch/frequency conversion.}
\[
f = f_{\text{root}} \cdot 2^{\,\text{semitones}/12}
\]
where $f_{\text{root}}$ is the mapping's current root frequency in Hz and $\text{semitones}$ the
signed offset applied to it, giving the equal-tempered result $f$.

\section{Development Iterations}
REMORA's architecture did not arrive fully formed; the node-graph engine described in
\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive} is the result of three iterations, summarised below, in which
generalisation followed rather than preceded hand-built mapping design.

\subsection*{Iteration 1: Sensing Pipeline}
The first iteration was firmware reading the IMU and streaming OSC, a minimal receiver on the
plugin side, and a single hardcoded tilt-to-pitch mapping. The goal was purely to prove the
sensor-to-sound pipeline end to end, not to explore expressivity.

\subsection*{Iteration 2: Hand-Built Mapping Library}
The eleven named mappings - Simple, Bowed Chord, Lead+Drone, Spin Filter, Bozendo V1/V2, Azimut and
its variants, Ben's, Spin Voices - were then built one at a time as their own C++ classes, each
adding a new gesture vocabulary (chords, Laban Effort, world-frame facing, transient detection) on
top of the shared \texttt{StaffMotionAnalyzer}.

\subsection*{Iteration 3: Generalising into the Node Graph}
Once eleven mappings existed as separate classes, a pattern became visible: every one of them was
really just arithmetic, shaping, and a handful of stateful primitives wired together in a fixed
pattern. The third iteration rebuilt the mapping layer as the small generic graph engine described
in \S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}, porting all eleven prior mappings over as presets rather than
deleting them.

\subsection*{Evidence from the Implemented System}
Claims (\ref{claim:dag}), (\ref{claim:presets}), and (\ref{claim:calibration}) are, at present, supported by the system as built and run by the author
during development rather than by an outside observer. The graph evaluator provisionally adds and
removes edges, recomputes a topological order, and drives synthesis every audio block without
recompilation or an audio dropout - this is exercised every time a preset is loaded or a wire is
changed during development, not a claim about untested code. Table~2's per-preset node counts are
read directly off the constructed graphs, so claim (\ref{claim:presets})'s "eleven mappings, one catalog" result is a
property of the code, not a reported impression. Claim (\ref{claim:calibration})'s mount-independence follows from the
two-stage calibration derivation (\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}) holding for any physically valid
sensor placement, which is a property we can state mathematically but have not yet stress-tested
by physically remounting the enclosure at several positions and re-running calibration at each.
Claim (\ref{claim:facing}) is the weakest of the four on current evidence: the facing heuristic runs correctly in
every development session, but "correctly" here means "matched the author's own sense of where the
staff was pointing," which is not a substitute for a logged, ground-truth comparison.

\subsection*{Planned Performer Evaluation}
To test the parts of these claims that are inherently about performer experience rather than code
behaviour - whether the graph is rewireable by someone other than its author, whether a
Laban-driven preset reads as more physically legible than a simple one, whether the node picker
scales to larger patches - we plan a semi-structured session with performers who have some staff,
baton, or movement-flow background but no prior exposure to REMORA. The intended structure: a short
calibration walkthrough; free play across a small span of existing presets chosen to range from a
minimal mapping to a fully Laban-driven one; a guided patching task (retuning an exposed parameter,
then rewiring which motion feature drives a sink); and a closing semi-structured interview.
Candidate measures include calibration time, time to complete the guided rewire unassisted, think-
aloud commentary during free play, a preference judgement between at least two presets with a
reason, and any node-picker search difficulties observed or reported. Each measure is chosen to
speak to a specific claim in Table~3 rather than to general usability. This protocol - not results -
is what we currently have; running it, with informed consent handled as described in the Ethical
Statement, is the immediate next step for this work.

\subsection*{Current Evidentiary Limitations}
To be explicit: no human-subject data has been collected for this instrument. Claims (\ref{claim:dag}), (\ref{claim:presets}), and
(\ref{claim:calibration}) currently rest on implementation-level evidence - the graph's own structure and behaviour under
the author's use - not on an independent performer's experience. Claim (\ref{claim:presets})'s catalog was grown
alongside the eleven mappings it reproduces, so its sufficiency for a mapping designed independently
of the project remains open (\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}). Claim (\ref{claim:facing}) is bounded to short sessions
by assumption pending a measurement that has not yet been made. None of the four claims should be
read as validated by user testing until the protocol above, or something like it, has actually been
run.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection*{Advantages and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The generalisation into a graph buys expressiveness and shareability - eleven previously hand-coded
mappings collapse into presets of one small dataflow language - at the cost of a patching step that
performers used to fixed instruments do not otherwise have to take. The limitations below are ones
the current build genuinely carries, not future work described in present tense.

\textbf{Network deployment is manual.} REMORA joins the host device's own Wi-Fi hotspot rather than
a dedicated access point, trading range and interference robustness for a one-step pairing setup.
That convenience hides a real cost: the hotspot SSID/password and the OSC destination IP are
constants compiled into the firmware. A phone hotspot does not guarantee the same IP across
sessions, and pairing with a different host network means a different SSID and password entirely -
either case currently requires editing the firmware source and reflashing the microcontroller over
USB before the staff will talk to the plugin again. There is no runtime Wi-Fi configuration, no
network discovery of the receiving host, and no feedback if the join fails silently.

\textbf{Single IMU.} All orientation and motion-quality features come from one IMU with no
magnetometer fusion; heading is deliberately kept relative to the calibration pose rather than
magnetic north, since the magnetometer is unreliable this close to the staff's own electronics and
typical stage rigs. That keeps facing free of magnetic interference, but it also means REMORA has no
absolute reference at all - any yaw drift accumulated since calibration is invisible and
uncorrected, and the facing feature is only ever as good as that session's calibration. We have not
measured drift quantitatively - no ground-truth heading reference has been logged alongside a
session - so rather than report a number we do not have, we explicitly bound claim (\ref{claim:facing}) to session
lengths on the order of tens of minutes of active play from a single calibration, matching the
durations used during development. That bound is an assumption carried over from informal use
during development, not a measured guarantee: nothing in the current implementation prevents drift
from growing unboundedly over a longer, uninterrupted rehearsal or set, and no
re-calibration-on-demand gesture currently exists to correct it mid-performance. No quantitative or
perceptual evaluation of facing or calibration accuracy has been carried out yet; it is one of the
open items the planned performer evaluation (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}) does not yet address either,
since ground-truth heading logging would need to be added to the protocol first.

\textbf{Graph interface complexity.} The node-graph editor is expressive for whoever is building or
modifying a mapping, but it exposes that same complexity to a performer who only wants to select and
play an existing preset: there is no simplified performance-only view that hides the graph until
it's asked for. As it stands, the interface serves instrument-builders and patch-authors more
directly than first-time performers, and whether that cost is acceptable is exactly the kind of
question the still-missing formal user study would need to answer.

\textbf{Remaining software robustness gaps.} A few implementation-level issues would need attention
before REMORA is exposed to inputs beyond the author's own testing: incoming OSC messages are read
without confirming their argument types match what the receiver expects, dropped or reordered
packets are not detected as such, and the handoff of a sensor snapshot from the network thread to
the audio thread retries in a tight spin loop rather than degrading gracefully under contention.

\textbf{Hardware.} The enclosure carries a 2000\,mAh LiPo battery, sized to comfortably outlast a
single rehearsal or set at the firmware's steady 100\,Hz sampling/transmission rate based on the
ESP32-S2's typical current draw, but runtime under REMORA's actual firmware has not been measured:
no discharge test has been logged to date. That measurement - ideally several runs to a defined
cutoff voltage, reported with variance rather than a single number - is needed before any runtime
claim can be made, and before the battery can be sized with confidence for a full day of
back-to-back performances without a recharge.

\subsection*{Future Improvements}

\textbf{Wireless transport.} The hotspot-join scheme in \S\ref{sec:limitations} could be replaced
with ESP-NOW: the staff-mounted ESP32 would send its sensor packets directly, peer-to-peer, to a
second ESP32 tethered over USB to the host computer, which forwards them to the plugin over serial
or a loopback OSC bridge. That removes the SSID/password/IP baked into firmware entirely - ESP-NOW
pairs by MAC address rather than joining a network - and sidesteps the "new hotspot means reflash"
problem, at the cost of adding a second microcontroller to the setup.

\textbf{Other extensions.} Gesture-triggered mode switching, a merged rest/motion mapping, a two-IMU
dual-tip configuration, further Laban Effort mapping targets, ML-based discrete gesture recognition,
and eventually exposing the node graph itself as a shareable/community patch format.

\section{Conclusions}
REMORA is a wireless, IMU-driven b\^o staff instrument, but its contribution is architectural rather
than being one more fixed gesture-to-sound mapping: the same small dataflow language - source,
math, and sink node primitives, evaluated as a DAG once per audio block and rewireable without
recompiling - is expressive enough to reproduce all eleven mapping strategies developed over the
course of the project as ordinary, live-editable presets (Table~2), from a minimal tilt-to-pitch
patch to the fully Laban-driven Bozendo and Ben's mappings. That claim is grounded in an explicit,
code-traceable account of mount-agnostic calibration, orientation tracking, and movement-quality
extraction (\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}), not asserted in the abstract alone.

What remains uncertain is exactly what \S\ref{sec:evaluation} makes explicit: whether a performer
other than the author finds the graph rewireable and the Laban-driven presets more legible, whether
the 63-type primitive catalog holds up against a mapping designed independently of it, and how much
heading drift accumulates beyond the session lengths used during development are all open questions
that implementation-level evidence alone cannot settle. The most important next step for this work
is therefore not another architectural extension but running the performer evaluation protocol
outlined in \S\ref{sec:evaluation}, so that the claims this paper makes about live-patchable,
Laban-grounded gestural control can be tested against use by someone other than the instrument's
own builder.

\section{Ethical Statement}
\textbf{Accessibility.} REMORA assumes a range of mobility and two-handed grip strength consistent
with swinging a b\^o staff; it is not currently adapted for performers who cannot support or swing a
1--1.5\,kg full-length staff, and no alternative mounting or reduced-effort variant has been
explored. This is a real limitation of the instrument's physical form, not merely of the present
build. That said, the skill barrier is not uniform across the preset catalog: presets such as Simple
ask only for basic tilt and roll and do not assume any prior staff-handling technique, while the
Laban-driven presets (Bozendo, Azimut) reward accumulated skill with the instrument's physical
vocabulary. Because every preset is authored in the same shared patching language
(\S\ref{sec:azimut-deep-dive}), a facilitator or performer can also build or select a reduced-effort
mapping for someone without a staff background, rather than being limited to the eleven built-in
presets. This is a partial mitigation on the skill axis, not a solution to the physical one above.

\textbf{Environmental impact.} The staff-mounted hardware consists of a 3D-printed/OpenSCAD-parametrised
enclosure, a rechargeable LiPo battery, an ESP32-S2 microcontroller, and an MPU-9250 IMU. The
enclosure's zip-tie mounting (\S\ref{sec:limitations}, The Build) avoids adhesives, so it can be
removed and the plastic reused or recycled without damaging the staff; the electronics are
similarly reusable across builds since nothing is glued in place. No lifecycle or embodied-carbon
analysis of the components has been performed.

\textbf{Socio-economic fairness.} REMORA is built entirely on free/libre and open-source tools -
JUCE, PlatformIO, and OpenSCAD - and the microcontroller and IMU are inexpensive, widely available
commodity parts. This keeps the reproduction cost and toolchain barrier low relative to
proprietary DMI development environments, which we consider a genuine point in the instrument's
favour under the NIME Code of Practice's socio-economic fairness criterion.

\textbf{Human participants.} No human-participant data has been collected to date; all reported
observations of the system in use are the first author's own. The performer evaluation outlined in
\S\ref{sec:evaluation} has not yet been conducted. When it is run, participants will give informed
consent before any session, be referred to only by anonymised identifiers, and be free to withdraw
at any point, consistent with this statement's other commitments.

\textbf{Use of Artificial Intelligence.} Per the NIME Code of Practice's guidance on AI use, we
disclose that a locally-run large language model (Mistral 7B) was used to help draft and edit
portions of this manuscript's prose under the first author's direction and review. This disclosure
applies only to the writing of this paper: REMORA's firmware, plugin, and mapping code were designed
and written entirely by the first author without AI code generation.
